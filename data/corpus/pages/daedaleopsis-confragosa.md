## Summary

Daedaleopsis confragosa, the blushing bracket or thin-walled maze polypore, is a bracket fungus in the family Polyporaceae, transferred to Daedaleopsis by Schröter in 1888. Its fruit bodies are tough, fan-shaped to semicircular shelves with a zoned brownish cap. They have white, maze-like to gill-like pores that bruise pink to red. Wikipedia describes it as common in northern temperate woodlands of Eurasia and eastern North America. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia states that the name 'blushing bracket' refers to the fungus's characteristic bruising reaction. It also records that James Bolton called it the 'rugged boletus'. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, it was first described as Boletus confragosus by the English naturalist James Bolton in his work An History of Fungusses, growing about Halifax, from specimens found on old trees near Fixby Hall and others sent from Darlington. It was later moved between several genera, including Daedalea (Persoon, 1801), Trametes (Rabenhorst, 1844), Polyporus (Kummer, 1871) and Lenzites (Patouillard, 1900). Schröter placed it in Daedaleopsis in 1888, and it is the type species of that genus. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the fruit bodies are used in ornamental paper making, where they are pulped, pressed and dried into sheets with unusual textures and colors. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes a lignicolous fungus causing white rot of sapwood, growing singly or in groups, sometimes in tiers, in wounds of living trees. Its preferred host is willow, but it also occurs on birch and other hardwoods. Wikipedia gives fruiting as usually June to December, with the hard shelves persisting year-round. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic on decaying hardwood logs and stumps, rarely from wounds of living hardwoods. It is partial to birch and willow, only rarely on oak and very rarely on conifer wood. It fruits summer through winter and is more commonly encountered east of the Rocky Mountains. In central Illinois, Kuo found it particularly on dying flowering dogwood (Cornus florida), and a nearly white form on elm and willow. A GBIF description records a specimen from a fallen angiosperm trunk in a broad-leaved forest in Yunnan, China. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, gbif_desc]*
