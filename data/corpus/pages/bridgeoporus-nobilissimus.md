## Summary

Bridgeoporus nobilissimus (W.B. Cooke) T.J. Volk, Burds. & Ammirati, combined in 1996 in the order Hymenochaetales, is known as the noble polypore and the fuzzy Sandozi. Wikipedia describes it as producing very large perennial fruit bodies, reported at up to 130 kg, on old conifers in the Pacific Northwest of North America. ## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the fungus was discovered in Clackamas County, Oregon, in 1943 by brothers Ali and Fred Sandoz. Foresters called it Fomes fuzzii-sandozii, a name said to refer to the collectors and the fuzzy surface of the conk. Cooke learned of the fungus in 1948 while visiting Daniel Elliot Stuntz, who kept a large fruit body that he and Alexander H. Smith had collected in Mount Rainier National Park. That specimen became the type collection. Cooke described it in 1949 as Oxyporus nobilissimus. Josiah Lincoln Lowe transferred it to Fomes in 1955. In 1996 Burdsall, Volk and Ammirati erected Bridgeoporus for it because of incompatibilities with both earlier placements. Wikipedia gives the main reason as pseudocystidia in this species, whereas Oxyporus has true cystidia. This account rests on a single source cluster. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia reports the species from the Pacific Northwest of North America, in the Cascade Range in Washington and Oregon, the Olympic Peninsula in Washington, and Redwood National Park in northern California. Fruit bodies are found singly or in overlapping layers on old, large-diameter trees, most often noble fir (Abies procera). More rarely they occur on Pacific silver fir (Abies amabilis) or western hemlock (Tsuga heterophylla), and once on a redwood snag. Wikipedia says molecular methods detected the fungus in Douglas fir, western hemlock and western redcedar, though fruit bodies have not been seen on those hosts. It is described as causing brown rot and as both parasitic and saprophytic. Fruit bodies are said not to occur on fallen logs or other dead wood without a root connection. Reported elevations are 300–1,220 m (1,000–4,000 ft). A Mycologia study sampled six populations in Oregon and Washington. The habitat details are from Wikipedia alone. *[wikipedia, lit_1]*
