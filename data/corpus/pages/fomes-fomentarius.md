## Summary

Fomes fomentarius (L.) Fr. is a member of the Polyporaceae. Wikipedia describes it as a perennial wood-decay polypore with large, hoof-shaped fruit bodies that grow on hardwood trunks and cause white rot. It is reported from Europe, Asia, Africa and North America. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet fomentarius is from the Latin fomentum, referring to tinder. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Linnaeus first described the fungus as Boletus fomentarius in his 1753 Species Plantarum. Lamarck moved it to Agaricus in 1783, and Meyer described it as Polyporus fomentarius in 1818, a name Fries sanctioned in 1821. Fries moved it to Fomes in his 1849 Summa vegetabilium Scandinaviae. Later attempts to place it in other genera (Placodes, Ochroporus, Scindalma, Ungulina, Elfvingia, Elfvingiella, Pyropolyporus) were unsuccessful. The darkest fruit bodies were once classified as Fomes nigricans, which Wikipedia says is now recognised as a synonym. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the flesh has traditionally been processed into amadou, used mainly as tinder. To make it, young fruit bodies are soaked, cut into strips, then beaten and stretched to separate the fibres. The same source says the flesh was also used to make caps, gloves and breeches, and that amadou is used in fly fishing for drying flies. The same article says the fungus was used as a firestarter in Hedeby and that use dates to at least 3000 BCE. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both note that the roughly 5,000-year-old Tyrolean Iceman (Ötzi) carried pieces of this fungus, apparently as tinder. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia reports that it most typically grows on hardwoods: birch is the most common host in northern areas, beech in the south, and oak in the Mediterranean. It has also been recorded on maple, cherry, hickory, lime, poplar, willow, alder, hornbeam and sycamore, and exceptionally on conifers. It usually grows alone, but several fruit bodies can occur on one trunk. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as parasitic and saprobic on hardwoods, especially birches and beech, on standing and fallen wood, growing alone or gregariously, and fairly widely distributed in northern and north-temperate North America. Wikipedia says fruit bodies are perennial and that the strongest growth period is early summer to autumn. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
