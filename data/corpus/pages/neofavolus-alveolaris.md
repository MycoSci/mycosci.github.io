## Summary

Neofavolus alveolaris (DC.) Sotome & T. Hatt. is a polypore in the family Polyporaceae, commonly called the hexagonal-pored polypore or diamond polypore. Wikipedia describes it as causing a white rot of dead hardwoods, with a yellowish to orange scaly cap and hexagonal or diamond-shaped pores. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as a small, stemmed polypore on hardwood sticks, and Wikipedia notes that North American material may belong to a separate species, N. americanus. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name is derived from the Greek meaning "many pores" and that the specific epithet alveolaris means "with small pits or hollows". This is a single-source statement, and it is not corroborated by any other supplied source. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, de Candolle first described the fungus in 1815 as Merulius alveolaris. Fries sanctioned it in 1821 as Cantharellus alveolaris. Bondartsev and Singer transferred it to Polyporus in 1941, and it was moved to Neofavolus in 2012. MushroomExpert.Com says there has long been confusion over the proper name and that Sotome et al. erected Neofavolus on molecular and morphological evidence. It adds that the combination may be officially invalid because the authors cited the wrong MycoBank number, and Wikispecies likewise flags the combination as nom. inval. for that reason. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, wikispecies]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes it as causing a white rot and growing singly or grouped on branches and twigs of hardwoods, commonly on shagbark hickory in spring and early summer. It lists reported hosts among dead hardwoods of the genera Acer, Castanea, Cornus, Corylus, Crataegus, Erica, Fagus, Fraxinus, Juglans, Magnolia, Morus, Populus, Pyrus, Robinia, Quercus, Syringa, Tilia and Ulmus. Wikipedia reports collections from Europe (Czechoslovakia, Italy, Portugal), China and Australia, and says eastern North American material may be N. americanus. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic on recently dead sticks and small logs of hardwoods with the bark still attached, occasionally on living branches. It says the fungus typically first appears in late spring but is frequently found in summer and fall, and is widely distributed and common east of the Rocky Mountains. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
