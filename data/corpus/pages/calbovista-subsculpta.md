## Summary

Calbovista subsculpta Morse ex M.T. Seidl (1995) is a puffball in the family Lycoperdaceae, known in English as the sculptured puffball. Wikipedia describes the fruit body as roughly round, up to 15 cm across, white becoming brownish with age, and covered in shallow pyramid-shaped plates or scales. It records the species as common in the Rocky Mountains and Pacific Coast ranges of western North America. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name Calbovista combines 'cal', referring to the puffball genus Calvatia, and 'bovista', alluding to the similarity to Bovista and Bovistella. It states that the epithet subsculpta refers to the species' resemblance to Calvatia sculpta, with which it had frequently been confused. This derivation comes from a single source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Elizabeth Eaton Morse noted in a 1935 Mycologia article that an abundant, widely distributed puffball of the western United States was commonly misidentified as Calvatia sculpta but differed in having extensively branched capillitial threads. Morse erected the genus Calbovista for it. Wikipedia gives the type collection as made at Soda Springs, California, in May 1934. Morse's publication was invalid because it lacked a Latin description, which the botanical code required from 1 January 1935. Wikipedia states that the genus and species were validly published with a Latin description by Michelle Seidl in 1995. It also states that A.H. Smith described the variety fumosa in 1965 from a collection in Kaniksu National Forest, Washington, and that this name was also invalid because it rested on an invalid genus. A 2013 Mycotaxon paper (lit_1) discusses the validation of the name Calbovista subsculpta var. fumosa. A 1935 Mycologia paper (lit_2) attributes Calbovista subsculpta to Morse (Mycologia 27: 97, 1935). *[wikipedia, lit_1, lit_2]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia, the only independent source with habitat text, describes the species as saprobic, decomposing dead plant material. Fruit bodies grow singly, in groups, or occasionally in clusters. The habitats given are areas of broken rocks mixed with soil, open coniferous forest at 900 to 3,400 m elevation, and road sides. East of the Cascade Range it is often found under ponderosa pine. Wikipedia lists collections from California, Colorado, Idaho, Washington, Wyoming and Oregon, with a range extending north to British Columbia and Alaska. It also says the species is often found by morel hunters in spring because it grows in similar habitats. Wikipedia gives inconsistent fruiting seasons: 'summer to autumn' in one passage and 'April to August' in another. *[wikipedia]*
