## Summary

Xylaria polymorpha (Pers.) Grev. 1824, commonly called dead man's fingers, is a saprobic fungus in the family Xylariaceae. It forms elongated, upright, club-shaped or strap-like stromata that turn from pale to dark brown or black as they mature. *[mushroomexpert, wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet polymorpha means "many forms", and relates this to the fungus's variable, often club-shaped fruiting body. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikispecies lists Sphaeria polymorpha Pers. (1797) as the original combination, with the current combination by Greville in 1824 (Flora edinensis). MushroomExpert.Com notes that the species name is often applied broadly, covering several species that are separated mainly on microscopic features. It also reports that Rogers and Callan (1986) documented two common morphological variants in the United States: a dull black northeastern and upper Midwestern form, and a browner southern and lower Midwestern form. Whether these are separate species has not been investigated with contemporary methods. *[wikispecies, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes it as a common inhabitant of forest and woodland areas, usually growing from the bases of rotting or injured tree stumps and decaying wood. It has also been recorded on woody legume pods, petioles and herbaceous stems. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic on decaying hardwood stumps and logs, usually at or near the base of the stump. It sometimes appears terrestrial but is attached to buried wood. It grows alone or, more commonly, in clusters, and causes a soft rot of the wood. According to that source it appears in spring and does not decay until late summer or fall, and it is common in North America from the Rocky Mountains eastward. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
