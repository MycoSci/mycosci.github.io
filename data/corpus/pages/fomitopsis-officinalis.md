## Summary

Fomitopsis officinalis (Vill.) Bondartsev & Singer, 1941, is a member of the Fomitopsidaceae. Wikipedia describes it as a wood-decay fungus that forms large conks on tree trunks and causes brown heart rot on conifers in Eurasia, Morocco and North America. Wikipedia also treats it under the name Laricifomes officinalis, which it says is favored on the basis of DNA analyses. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the species epithet officinalis denotes an organism associated with herbalism or medicine. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia gives agarikon, eburiko and quinine conk as common names. It says 'quinine conk' refers to the fungus's bitter taste, and that the conks were collected extensively to produce medicinal quinine, which they were thought to contain because of that taste. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia says DNA analyses showed this species to be genetically distant from Fomitopsis, and that the name Laricifomes officinalis is therefore favored. On that account it is the only member of the genus Laricifomes. This is a single-source account and does not describe the original material or the earlier transfers. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the fungus, called 'bread of the ghosts' in local languages, was important medicinally and spiritually to Pacific Northwest Coast peoples such as the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian. Fruiting bodies were carved into masks, most likely for ritual purposes, and often marked the graves of tribal shamans. The same article says there is evidence that mycelium growing in rotting wood was processed into a leather-like textile. It also states that the Ancient Greeks used the fungus medicinally, citing Dioscorides in 65 CE. All of this comes from one source, Wikipedia. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that the fungus lives predominantly in old-growth forests in Eurasia, Morocco and North America. It commonly prefers Larix species but has also been observed on some conifers in the genera Pinus and Cedrus. The conks grow from the side of the trunk or hang from branches, as high as 20 metres above the ground. Wikipedia says spores are released through the underside of the fruiting body during warmer months. *[wikipedia]*
