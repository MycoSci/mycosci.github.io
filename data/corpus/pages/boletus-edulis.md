## Summary

Boletus edulis Bull. (1782) is a bolete in the family Boletaceae and the type species of the genus Boletus. Wikipedia describes a large brown cap, tubes rather than gills, and a stout stipe with a raised network pattern (reticulation). It is reported from Eurasia and North America. MushroomExpert.Com notes that whether the 'true' B. edulis occurs in North America is debated, because edulis-like species there are still being sorted out. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the generic name derives from Latin bōlētus 'mushroom', borrowed from Ancient Greek βωλίτης 'terrestrial fungus', which ultimately comes from bōlos/βῶλος 'lump' or 'clod'. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

According to Wikipedia, the Italian name porcino ('porcine') echoes the Roman term suilli ('hog mushrooms'), and the derivation has been ascribed to the resemblance of young fruit bodies to piglets. The English 'penny bun' refers to its rounded brownish shape. The German Steinpilz ('stone mushroom') refers to the species' firm flesh. 'Cep' comes from the Catalan cep or French cèpe. 'Cèpe de Bordeaux' derives from the Gascon cep 'trunk', for the fat stalk. The Swedish name Karljohanssvamp honours King Charles XIV John, who popularised the species in Sweden after 1818. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that Pierre Bulliard described B. edulis in 1782 and that it still bears its original name. Because Bulliard's work predates the 1821 starting date for fungal taxonomy that applied at the time, the name originally needed sanction by Fries and was written Boletus edulis Bull.:Fr. A 1987 revision of the botanical code moved the starting date to 1753, so that ratification is no longer needed. Early alternate names include Boletus solidus (Sowerby, 1809) and Gray's Leccinum edule, and Gray's transfer to Leccinum was later judged inconsistent with the nomenclatural rules. Wikispecies lists further synonyms. *[wikipedia, wikispecies]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes B. edulis as ectomycorrhizal, occurring in deciduous and coniferous forests and plantations. It lists pine, spruce, hemlock and fir as dominant host trees, with chestnut, chinquapin, beech and oak among other hosts. It reports fruit bodies growing singly or in small clusters, appearing from summer to autumn (June to November in the UK). In North America it reports May to October inland and August to January on the West Coast. It gives a Northern Hemisphere distribution across Eurasia and North America and says the species does not occur naturally in the Southern Hemisphere, where it has been introduced. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as associating with spruces and, depending on how the species is defined, various hardwoods, and notes that its North American identity is uncertain. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
