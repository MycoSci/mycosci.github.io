## Summary

Leucocoprinus fragilissimus (Ravenel ex Berk. & M.A. Curtis) Pat., 1900, commonly called the fragile dapperling, is a gilled mushroom in the family Agaricaceae. Wikipedia describes it as a small dapperling with very thin, extremely fragile, yellow flesh, and MushroomExpert.Com likewise calls it a tiny, exceedingly fragile lepiotoid mushroom whose cap begins to dissolve and collapse within a few hours of expanding. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia (the only source that tells the naming history, and which notes that the history is complex), Hiatula fragilissima was described in 1853 by Berkeley and Curtis, based on an earlier unofficial classification by Henry William Ravenel. Patouillard had already transferred it to Leucocoprinus as L. fragilissimus in 1900, and Morgan's 1907 transfer to Lepiota came after that. Wikipedia also reports that a separate name, Agaricus licmophorus, was described by Berkeley and Broome in 1871 and later moved through Lepiota, Hiatula and Leucocoprinus, and that Pegler reclassified it as L. fragilissimus in 1972. It gives 1805 as the date of the earliest description under the name Agaricus flammula, by Albertini and Schweinitz. Wikipedia attributes the many competing names to the species being described repeatedly from greenhouses and plant pots in Europe, with only written descriptions and illustrations to compare. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

MushroomExpert.Com describes the species as saprobic, growing alone or scattered in humus in summer, and reports it from the southeastern United States (Texas to Florida, Maryland and southern Ohio, and the southern Appalachians) and also from Costa Rica. Its illustrated and described collection is from Ohio. Wikipedia describes it as a saprotroph on very decayed plant matter (humus or compost), growing individually or sparsely in wooded areas. Wikipedia lists it from southern North America, South America, southern Europe, Africa, southern and eastern Asia, Australia and New Zealand. Wikipedia also relays historical records: growing in tanbark in the Ghent Botanical Garden greenhouses in August and September (Kickx, 1867); on the ground at Peradeniya, Sri Lanka, and also in hothouses (Berkeley and Broome, 1871); in small tufts in greenhouses in France in summer and autumn (Gillet, 1874); and on earth and decayed vegetation at the edges of swamps in South Carolina (Morgan, 1907). A GBIF description record lists the substrate as soil ("Suelo") and the habit as terrestrial. *[mushroomexpert, wikipedia, gbif_desc]*
