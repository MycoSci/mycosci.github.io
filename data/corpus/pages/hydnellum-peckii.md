## Summary

Hydnellum peckii Banker is a stipitate hydnoid fungus in the family Bankeraceae, with its type locality in New York. Wikipedia describes young fruit bodies that exude bright red droplets, which gave rise to common names such as bleeding tooth fungus. It is described as mycorrhizal with conifers and is recorded in North America and Europe. Wikipedia reports it as considered inedible. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet honors the mycologist Charles Horton Peck. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the unusual appearance of the young fruit bodies earned the species several descriptive common names, including strawberries and cream, bleeding Hydnellum, bleeding tooth fungus, red-juice tooth and Devil's tooth. It also records the name "Peck's hydnum". It compares the fruit bodies to "Danish pastry topped with strawberry jam". *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia credits the American mycologist Howard James Banker with the first scientific description. It says Saccardo placed the species in Hydnum (1925) and Snell and Dick placed it in Calodon (1956). Wikipedia also notes that Banker's original description compared the odor to hickory nuts. This is a single-source account (Wikipedia); no primary literature was supplied. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia states that the fruit bodies of this and other Hydnellum species are prized by mushroom dyers, with colors ranging from beige without a mordant to various shades of blue or green depending on the mordant used. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes it as an ectomycorrhizal fungus, found solitary, scattered or clustered on the ground under conifers, often among mosses and pine needle litter. It names Picea abies and Pinus sylvestris as primary hosts. In the Puget Sound area of Washington it is reported with Douglas-fir, fir and hemlock, and on the Oregon coast under lodgepole pine. Wikipedia also notes a preference for mountainous or subalpine ecosystems and for acidic, moss-covered soils. These habitat details come from a single source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*
