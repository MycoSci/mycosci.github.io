## Summary

Boletellus ananas (M.A. Curtis) Murrill, 1909, is a bolete in the family Boletaceae and the type species of the genus Boletellus. Wikipedia gives its common name as the pineapple bolete and describes a shaggy, scaly, red-pink cap, a yellow pore surface that bruises blue, and a partial veil that hangs from the cap margin as remnants. The spine records occurrences in the United States, Mexico, Central America, parts of South America, Asia, Australia and New Zealand. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name Boletellus means "small boletus", and that the epithet ananas alludes to the word for pineapple, referring to the pineapple-like pattern of scales on the cap surface. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Moses Ashley Curtis first named the species Boletus ananas in 1848 from specimens found near the Santee River, South Carolina. In 1909 William Murrill described the genus Boletellus and made B. ananas its type species. Murrill treated Boletus isabellinus Peck (1897) as a synonym, but Wally Snell doubted this in 1933. Rolf Singer and colleagues (1992) suggested the name Boletellus coccineus. Wikipedia says Singer's name rested on a mistaken belief about the earliest available name, because Fries's Boletus coccineus is an illegitimate later homonym. Wikipedia concludes that Boletus ananas M.A. Curtis 1848 is the earliest available name and is the basionym. This account comes from a single source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia reports that the fruit bodies grow scattered or in groups under oak and pine, often at the tree bases. It also reports that in Guyana the species fruits on the trunks of Dicymbe corymbosa, and that in southeastern North America and Central America collections are associated with Pinus and Quercus. Its ectomycorrhizal status has been debated because it often fruits on wood. Wikipedia gives the North American season as June to October. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) calls the ecology "Uncertain": possibly mycorrhizal with pines, but often reported growing from pine wood and roots and so possibly saprobic. Kuo gives spring, summer and fall as the season. The collections he studied came from Georgia, Florida and North Carolina and were all associated with dead pines and oaks. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
