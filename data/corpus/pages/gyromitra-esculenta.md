## Summary

Gyromitra esculenta (Pers. ex Fr.) is a brain-shaped ascomycete in the family Discinaceae, described by Persoon in 1800 and placed in Gyromitra by Fries in 1849. The sources describe it as widely distributed across Europe and North America, fruiting in spring and early summer. Sources disagree on whether it is an edible delicacy or a dangerous toxic species; see the disagreements and the toxicity section before treating it as food. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name is derived from the Greek gyros 'round' and mitra 'headband', and that the specific epithet is derived from the Latin esculentus, 'edible'. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia states that G. esculenta belongs to a group known as 'false morels', so named for their resemblance to the true morels of the genus Morchella. It also notes that the German term 'lorchel' dates from the 19th century and derives from the older 'lorche', itself from the 18th-century Low German 'Lorken'. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the fungus was first described in 1800 by Christian Hendrik Persoon as Helvella esculenta. Elias Magnus Fries placed it in the genus Gyromitra in 1849. The genus was classically placed in the Helvellaceae, but ribosomal DNA analysis showed the false morels to be only distantly related to Helvella and closest to Discina. The genera Gyromitra, Discina, Pseudorhizina and Hydnotrya are now placed together in the Discinaceae. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the species is marketed and consumed in several European countries and in parts of North America, including the Great Lakes region. It is traditionally considered a delicacy in parts of Spain and is highly regarded in Finland and Bulgaria. Sale is restricted or prohibited in Germany, Switzerland and Spain, and Finnish sale requires a legally mandated poison warning and preparation instructions. Wikipedia also reports that in 2025 the Finnish Food Agency removed its status as an edible mushroom. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultivation

Wikipedia reports that enthusiasts in Finland have buried newspaper inoculated with the fungus in autumn and returned the following spring to collect mushrooms. It also reports that the fungus has been grown to fruiting in culture, and that strains with much lower gyromitrin concentrations have been discovered. Wikipedia presents this as scope for future research, not as an established cultivation method. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that it grows on sandy soil in temperate coniferous forest and occasionally in deciduous woodland. Among conifers it is mostly found under pines (Pinus), and sometimes under aspens (Populus). The stated hunting period is April to July, and it is reported more often where the ground has been disturbed (clearings, washes, forest fire clearings, roadsides). MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as found in spring, primarily under conifers, and widely distributed in northern and montane North America. It calls the species officially saprobic but potentially also mycorrhizal. Mushroom World describes it as growing solitary or in small groups near conifers, typically on acid, sandy soils. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, mushroomworld]*

Frequent consumption is reported to cause hepatitis and neurological disease (lit_1).
