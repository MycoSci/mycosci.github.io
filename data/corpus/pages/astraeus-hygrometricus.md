## Summary

Astraeus hygrometricus (Pers.) Morgan, 1889, is a gasteroid fungus in the Boletales, family Diplocystidiaceae, known as the hygroscopic, barometer or false earthstar. Despite its earthstar shape it is not closely related to the true earthstars (Geastrum). Its rays open and close with moisture, and Wikipedia reports that it is now thought to be restricted to Southern Europe after several regional populations were split off as separate species. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

According to Wikipedia, the specific name is derived from the Greek words hygros ('wet') and metron ('measure'). *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia states that the colloquial names 'hygrometer earthstar', 'hygroscopic earthstar', 'barometer earthstar' and 'water-measure earthstar' allude to the fungus's hygroscopic behavior, while 'false earthstar' reflects its resemblance to Geastrum species (true earthstars). *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Per Wikipedia, Persoon described the species in 1801 as Geastrum hygrometricum (spelled Geaster in the source text). Andrew P. Morgan set it as the type species of his new genus Astraeus in 1889, citing differences from Geastrum: no open chambers in the young gleba, larger and branched capillitium threads, no true hymenium, and larger spores. Some authorities continued to place it in Geastrum, and Cunningham explicitly transferred it back in 1944, but later authorities largely treated Astraeus as distinct. In the 2000s and 2013, molecular studies split several Asian and North American populations formerly called A. hygrometricus into separate species. Wikipedia reports that preliminary work suggests the type population is restricted to Europe. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) is sharply critical of the 2013 North American species paper (Phosri et al.) and says its new names need reconsideration before they can be applied accurately. This is a single-author critique. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the German Mycological Society chose the species as its 'Mushroom of the Year' in 2005. It says the species has been used in traditional Chinese medicine as a hemostatic, with the spore dust applied externally to stop wound bleeding and reduce chilblains. It also says the Baiga and Bharia of Madhya Pradesh have been reported to use the fruit bodies medicinally, blending the spore mass with mustard seed oil as a salve for burns. Finally, it says the Blackfoot of North America called the fungus 'fallen stars', considering them stars fallen to earth during supernatural events. All of this is single-source (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes A. hygrometricus as an ectomycorrhizal fungus associated with a broad range of trees, found on the ground in open fields, scattered or in groups, especially in nutrient-poor, sandy or loamy soils. It says that in North America associations with oak and pine are usual, and that in India it commonly grows with chir pine (Pinus roxburghii) and sal (Shorea robusta). It reports occasional growth on rocks, preferring acid substrates such as slate and granite and avoiding lime-rich ones. Fruit bodies typically appear in autumn, and dry fruit bodies persist, possibly for several years. MushroomExpert.Com separately describes it as mycorrhizal, growing alone, scattered or gregariously in sandy soil, especially disturbed ground, near hardwoods or conifers, in summer and fall (over winter in warmer climates). Kuo's collections are from Michigan, New Jersey, North Carolina and Texas. Because the species has been split taxonomically, it is unclear how much of this applies to European material as opposed to populations now assigned to other names. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
