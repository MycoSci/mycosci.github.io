## Summary

Chorioactis geaster (Peck) Kupfer ex Eckblad, 1968, is the only species in the genus Chorioactis, placed in the family Chorioactidaceae (Pezizales) and known as the Texas star or devil's cigar. Its fruit body opens from a dark, cigar-like or football-shaped closed form into a star of leathery rays. It has a disjunct distribution, with records from Texas, Oklahoma, Japan and Taiwan. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

The specific epithet geaster alludes to members of the genus Geastrum, which also open into star-shaped fruit bodies commonly called earthstars (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia quotes the mycologist Fred Jay Seaver on 'devil's cigar', who could not say whether the name refers to the bloated-cigar form and colour of young specimens or to the fungus appearing to 'smoke' at maturity. The Japanese name kirinomitake is said to come from the resemblance of the unopened fruit body to the seed pods of kiri, the empress tree (Paulownia tomentosa). The sources give no origin for 'Texas star'. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that the fungus was first collected in 1893 by the botanist Lucien Marcus Underwood, who sent specimens to Charles Horton Peck. Peck described it as Urnula geaster in the Annual Report of the New York State Botanist, though he doubted its placement in Urnula. In 1902 the student mycologist Elsie Kupfer, working with Underwood, compared the microscopic structure of the hymenium with that of similar species and proposed the new genus Chorioactis for it. Later authors, including Heald and Wolf (1910) and Seaver (1928, 1942), opposed the change. Finn-Egil Eckblad established Chorioactis as a valid genus in 1968 in his monograph on the Discomycetes. Wikispecies lists Urnula geaster Peck as the basionym. *[wikipedia, wikispecies]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that a 1997 bill to make the Texas star the official state fungus of Texas passed the Texas Senate but failed in the House. The Texas Legislature designated it the official 'State Mushroom of Texas' in 2021. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

In Texas, Wikipedia reports fruit bodies growing singly or in groups from the roots and stumps of cedar elm (Ulmus crassifolia), usually appearing between October and April. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) independently describes central Texas collections growing scattered to gregariously on or very near cedar elm stumps, October through January, and calls the fungus 'apparently saprobic'. Wikipedia says the fungus is believed to be saprobic but that this is not known definitively. For Japan, Wikipedia gives dead oak as the usual host. Wikipedia lists records from Texas, from Choctaw County, Oklahoma (reported 2017), from Japan, and from Taiwan (discovered 2023). *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
