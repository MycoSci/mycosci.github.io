## Summary

Chalciporus piperatus (Bull.) Bataille, the peppery bolete, is a species in the family Boletaceae, first described as Boletus piperatus and placed in Chalciporus in 1908. Wikipedia describes it as a small bolete with an orange-fawn cap, brownish pores and a very peppery-tasting flesh. It is recorded from mixed woodland in Europe and North America, and has also been recorded under introduced trees elsewhere. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the species name piperatus means "peppery" in Latin, and that the species is commonly known as the "peppery bolete". *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Pierre Bulliard described the species as Boletus piperatus in 1790. It was later transferred to Leccinum (Gray, 1821), Viscipellis (Quélet, 1886), Ixocomus (Quélet, 1888), Suillus (Kuntze, 1898) and Ceriomyces (Murrill, 1909). Frédéric Bataille gave it its current name in 1908, making it the type species of his newly circumscribed genus Chalciporus. Wikipedia also says two varieties have been described, var. hypochryseus and var. amarellus, and that authorities disagree on whether amarellus has independent taxonomic significance. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the fruit bodies have been used as a condiment in many countries, with the chef Antonio Carluccio recommending it only to add peppery flavour to other mushrooms. It also reports that the fruit bodies can be used for mushroom dyeing, giving yellow, orange or greenish-brown dyes depending on the mordant. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that fruit bodies occur singly, scattered or in groups on the ground, naturally in or near coniferous or beech and oak woodlands, often on sandy soils. It gives fruiting times of August to November in Europe, July to October in the eastern United States and September to January on the Pacific Coast. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as mycorrhizal, primarily with conifers but well documented under aspen and other hardwoods, growing alone, scattered or gregariously in summer and fall. Wikipedia notes that the fungus is not confirmed as ectomycorrhizal and that some speculate it is a mycoparasite on the mycorrhiza of Amanita muscaria. It also reports the species growing under introduced Pinus taeda in southern Brazil and naturalised in Tasmania and Victoria with Nothofagus cunninghamii. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
