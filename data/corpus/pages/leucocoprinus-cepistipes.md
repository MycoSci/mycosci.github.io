## Summary

Leucocoprinus cepistipes (Sowerby) Pat. is a species in the family Agaricaceae, known in English as the onion-stalk parasol. The name Leucocoprinus cepistipes dates to 1889. Wikipedia describes it as typically found on wood debris such as wood chips, and also in potted plants and greenhouses. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet cepistipes (originally cepaestipes) derives from the Latin cepae, 'onions', and stipes, 'stalk or stem', giving 'onion stem'. It presents this as a reference to the bulbous stem base. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the common name onion-stalk parasol refers to the bulbous stem base. It also reports that in 1821 Samuel Frederick Gray suggested the common name 'onion-stalked dung-stool' when he placed the species in Coprinus. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, James Sowerby described the species in 1796 as Agaricus cepistipes (or 'cepaestipes') from bark beds around London, where he called it 'not uncommon'. Wikipedia reports later transfers by Samuel Frederick Gray in 1821 (Coprinus cepaestipes) and by Otto Kuntze in 1871 (Lepiota cepaestipes). In 1889 Narcisse Théophile Patouillard reclassified it as Leucocoprinus cepistipes, from specimens collected in March and April 1889 on an old rotten coconut tree trunk in Martinique. Wikipedia adds that early descriptions often conflated this species with yellow and white Leucocoprinus species, so some historical records may refer to other species. This account rests on a single independent source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states the species is typically found on wood debris, such as wood chips, but may also grow in potted plants or greenhouses. It says the species has commonly been described from greenhouses and hothouses and is especially noted for growing in bark beds. Wikipedia reports that in 1867 Jean Kickx documented it growing in tanbark in the greenhouses of the Ghent Botanical Garden during the summer. Patouillard's 1889 Martinique specimens came from an old rotten coconut tree trunk. These statements come from one source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*
