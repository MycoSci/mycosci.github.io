## Summary

Mycena purpureofusca (Peck) Sacc., the purple edge bonnet, is an agaric in the family Mycenaceae. Charles Horton Peck described it as Agaricus purpureofuscus in 1885, and Saccardo transferred it to Mycena in 1887. Wikipedia describes conical to bell-shaped purple caps up to 2.5 cm across with dark greyish-purple gill edges, growing on decaying conifer wood and debris in Europe and North America. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet purpureofuscus combines the Latin words purpur (purple) and fusco (dark or dusky). It also says the mushroom is named for the dark greyish-purple color of its gill edges. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Peck described the species as Agaricus purpureofuscus in 1885, from a type collection made at Caroga, New York, on a moss-covered spruce trunk. Saccardo transferred it to Mycena in 1887, and Murrill moved it to Prunulus in 1916; that genus has since been subsumed in Mycena. Petter Karsten described a Scandinavian collection as Mycena atromarginata var. fuscopurpurea in 1879, and Maas Geesteranus later placed it in synonymy with M. purpureofusca. Maas Geesteranus also treated Mycena sulcata, described by Velenovský in 1920 from Czechoslovakia, as a synonym. Alexander H. Smith placed the species in section Calodontes, subsection Ciliatae in his 1947 monograph on North American Mycena. Rolf Singer placed it in section Rubromarginata in 1986. All of this comes from a single independent source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that the fruit bodies grow singly or in clusters on the decaying wood of conifers, particularly spruce, pine and Douglas-fir, and that the species is commonly found on decaying pine cones. In a European study it was found on logs from an early stage of decay (wood mostly hard, most of the bark remaining) through to logs decayed until mostly soft throughout. Wikipedia reports that Smith noted Michigan collections are likely to be found on old hemlock knots lying in the soil, where it usually fruits singly, and that it tends to grow in clusters on logs and stumps. It also states that in the UK the fungus is commonly found in Caledonian pine woods and is considered an indicator species for that habitat type. Wikipedia lists North American records from North Carolina, Tennessee, New York, Michigan, Montana, Idaho, Washington, Oregon, California, Virginia and South Dakota, and from Ontario in Canada. European records are from Britain, Scotland, the Czech Republic, Poland, Germany, Turkey and Austria. The GBIF description record lists distribution entries for Belgium, Denmark, Norway, Sweden and Vermont, without habitat text. *[wikipedia, gbif_desc]*
