## Summary

Hydnellum fuligineoviolaceum (Kalchbr.) E. Larss., K.H. Larss. & Kõljalg (2019) is a tooth fungus in the family Bankeraceae, order Thelephorales. Wikipedia states that it is found in northern Europe in association with conifers, particularly Scots pine, and that the IUCN lists it as Vulnerable because of habitat loss. Wikipedia also states that, as of 2024, no genetic sequences are known from outside northern Europe, and that specimens assigned to this name elsewhere almost certainly represent distinct taxa. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that Károly Kalchbrenner first described the species in 1874 as Hydnum fuligineoviolaceum, in Elias Fries's Hymenomycetes europaei. The GBIF record gives the same basionym and publication, and lists a holotype from Olaszi, Prešov Region, Slovakia (UPS F-173546). According to Wikipedia, Narcisse Théophile Patouillard transferred it to Sarcodon in 1900. Rudolph Arnold Maas Geesteranus published Sarcodon talpa in 1967, which is now treated as a synonym. Wikipedia says molecular studies reinstated the species in Hydnellum, reflecting a narrower generic concept supported by DNA analyses. The GBIF record lists the combination in Hydnellum as comb. nov. by E. Larsson, K.H. Larsson & Kõljalg. The Wikipedia account is a single-source claim, as the other Wikipedia-derived source (iNaturalist) is not independent. *[wikipedia, gbif_desc]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia, the only independent source with descriptive text, says the species forms ectomycorrhizal associations mainly with members of the Pinaceae. It names Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) as the most frequent host, occasionally silver fir (Abies alba) and Greek fir (A. cephalonica), and possibly Norway spruce (Picea abies). It is described as specialising on nutrient-poor, dry calcareous soils with an acidic litter layer and minimal humus. Wikipedia says it inhabits mature pine forests in Mediterranean and temperate regions, can occur under fir in alpine zones, and in northern Europe favours older, open pine stands. Wikipedia names Norway, Spain and Sweden as the locations of its largest subpopulations. It reports the species as IUCN Vulnerable, with an estimated decline in habitat quality and area of more than 30% over 50 years, and cites clearcutting of old calcareous pine stands as a main threat. No source supplied states a fruiting season. *[wikipedia]*
