## Discovery and naming

Wikispecies gives the basionym as Armillaria matsutake S. Ito & S. Imai, published in 1925 in Botanical Magazine [Tokyo] 39(468), with the host/locality recorded as "On soil in woodland: Japan". The combination Tricholoma matsutake was made by Singer in Annales Mycologici 41 (1943). Wikispecies lists Armillaria nauseosa A. Blytt (1905), Tricholoma nauseosum (A. Blytt) Kytöv. (1989) and Tricholoma caligatum var. nauseosum (A. Blytt) Bon (1990) as heterotypic synonyms. Wikipedia adds that a 2000 report categorized T. nauseosum as a synonym of T. matsutake. The supplied sources do not say why the transfers were made. *[wikispecies, wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that in the 1940s the abundance of matsutake in Japan made them accessible to the general public after long being considered a luxury good. After the decline of the mushrooms there, international trade created a fluctuating market, at times very lucrative for regions where they grow, such as Yunnan Province in southwest China. Wikipedia states that the annual Japanese harvest is now under 1,000 tons and that the Japanese supply is largely made up by imports from China, Korea, the Pacific Northwest, British Columbia and northern Europe. This is a single-source account. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes matsutake as a mycorrhizal species that grows under trees, usually concealed under litter on the forest floor, forming a symbiotic relationship with the roots of various tree species, typically in fir and pine forests. In Korea and Japan it is most commonly associated with Pinus densiflora. In northeast China its growth is said to depend on P. densiflora, with the same distribution. Wikipedia lists its range as East Asia, Bhutan, Laos, parts of Europe (Estonia, Finland, Norway, Poland, Sweden) and northern North America, "especially the Pacific Northwest around November". The Wikispecies record for the basionym gives "On soil in woodland: Japan". Wikipedia also states that specific growth requirements, rarity of suitable forest and terrain, and competition from wildlife and insects make the mushrooms hard to find. It attributes reduced Japanese production to the pine-killing nematode Bursaphelenchus xylophilus. *[wikipedia, wikispecies]*
