## Summary

Amanita smithiana Bas (1969, Amanitaceae) has its type locality in Washington. Wikipedia describes it as a species of agaric found in coniferous and broadleaved woodland in the Pacific Northwest of North America. Clinical and toxicological literature documents it as a nephrotoxic mushroom: poisonings begin with gastrointestinal symptoms and are followed by acute renal failure, and cases have required hemodialysis. It is reported to be easily mistaken for the matsutake. *[wikipedia, lit_1, lit_2]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet honors the mycologist Alexander H. Smith, who collected the type specimens from Washington in 1941. This is a single-source statement. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that the species was described by the Dutch mycologist Cornelis Bas in 1969, from type specimens that Alexander H. Smith collected in Washington in 1941, and that it belongs in the subgenus Lepidella. MushroomExpert.Com likewise places it in Section Lepidella of Amanita and cites Bas, 1969. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that it is found on soil in coniferous (Abies, Tsuga, Pseudotsuga) and broadleaved (Alnus, Quercus) woodland in the Pacific Northwest of North America, and that it fruits in August and September. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as mycorrhizal with conifers, especially Douglas-fir, often reported near decaying wood, distributed from the Pacific Northwest to central California, and fruiting in fall and winter. The two sources give different fruiting seasons. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

- **Edibility:** poisonous *[wikipedia, lit_1, lit_2]*
- Wikipedia, a 2013 Clinical Toxicology case report and a 1998 Academic Emergency Medicine case series all describe poisonings in people who believed they had picked the edible matsutake. The case series reports four patients who presented in acute renal failure 4 to 6 days after ingestion and underwent hemodialysis for at least several weeks before returning to baseline renal function. The 2013 case report describes a 63-year-old who presented with anuria and required hemodialysis, and who recovered renal function. *[wikipedia, lit_1, lit_2]*
- **Toxicity:** An initial gastrointestinal phase is followed by a renal phase of acute kidney injury or acute renal failure (described as acute nephritis by Wikipedia).
  - onset: Gastrointestinal symptoms began from 20 minutes to 12 hours after ingestion in one case series and 1 to 12 hours in Wikipedia. Renal failure presented 4 to 6 days after ingestion in the case series and after a delay of 2 to 6 days in Wikipedia.
  - severity: Acute renal failure requiring hemodialysis is documented in the case report and the case series. In the cases described, renal function returned, including after several weeks of dialysis.
  - The sources disagree on the toxin. Wikipedia says toxicity is thought to come from chlorocrotylglycine and allenic norleucine. The 2013 case report says the chemical structure of the toxin is unknown. A 1994 Mycologia study found no orellanine and isolated a toxin with the chemical properties of an amino sugar, with a shorter latency period than orellanine. The 2013 report says thin-layer chromatography can help confirm the diagnosis. *[wikipedia, lit_1, lit_2, lit_3]*

### Look-alikes
