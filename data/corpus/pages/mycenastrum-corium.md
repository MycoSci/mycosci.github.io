## Summary

Mycenastrum corium (Guers.) Desv. (1842) is a puffball in the family Agaricaceae, listed in the catalog under the common name Corkstar Puffball. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both describe a stemless, ball-shaped puffball whose thick, tough skin breaks up irregularly with age. Wikipedia describes it as widely distributed but a threatened species in Europe. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first described in 1805 as Lycoperdon corium in Flore Française by de Candolle and Lamarck, who attributed it to the French botanist Louis Ben Guersent. Guersent is said to have found it in an alfalfa field between La Sotte and Rouen in northern France. Nicaise Auguste Desvaux gave it its current name in 1842, when he circumscribed the genus Mycenastrum to contain it. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both mention a subspecies, M. corium subsp. ferrugineum, described in 2005 from Colorado, with a rusty red to orange interior. Both also mention a variety, M. corium var. diabolicum, described from South America, with exceptionally spiny capillitial threads. Wikipedia credits the variety to Homrich & Wright (1973) and the subspecies to Orson K. Miller. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the thick outer peridium lets the puffballs withstand hard blows without breaking, and that children have used them as replacements for balls. It also reports medicinal use in Mexico as a hemostatic, as a throat and lung tonic, and for purported anti-inflammatory properties. This is a single-source account. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes M. corium as saprobic. It says the fruit bodies grow on the ground singly, scattered, in rings or in clusters, and can also grow underground. It reports fruiting at low elevations in open habitats dominated by sagebrush and saltbush, or in grassy or shrubby wet areas in dry prairie. Other habitats it lists are old haystacks, silage and roadsides. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic and terrestrial, growing alone or gregariously in grassy areas, especially manure-rich meadows and pastures but also lawns, in summer and fall. Kuo notes that it is almost universally reported in association with horse or cow manure, but his own illustrated collections came from yards where no livestock is kept. He describes it as fairly common in the Great Plains, the Rocky Mountains and western North America, and occasional in eastern North America. Wikipedia says it is most common in western North America and is a threatened species in Europe. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
