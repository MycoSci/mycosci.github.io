## Summary

Marasmius rotula (Scop.) Fr. is a small agaric in the family Marasmiaceae, commonly called the collared parachute or pinwheel mushroom. It is the type species of the genus Marasmius. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com describe it as a tiny, whitish, pleated-capped mushroom with a wiry dark stem, and its gills attach to a collar encircling the stem. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet is a diminutive of rota, the Latin word for 'wheel'. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the name 'little wheel fungus' suggests the collar to which the gills are attached like the spokes of a wheel. It says 'horse hair fungus' is a name shared with other Marasmius species, and that Samuel Frederick Gray called the species the 'collared dimple-stool'. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Giovanni Antonio Scopoli first described the species as Agaricus rotula in 1772. Elias Fries redescribed it in Systema Mycologicum in 1821 and transferred it to Marasmius in his 1838 Epicrisis Systematis Mycologici. Later transfers to Androsaceus (Patouillard, 1887) and Chamaeceras (Kuntze, 1898) produced synonyms, and both genera are now sunk into Marasmius. The genus name Marasmius, with M. rotula as lectotype, was conserved over Micromphale at the 1954 Paris Congress on Botanical Nomenclature. Wikipedia also notes that varieties fuscus, microcephalus and phyllophyla were described. It says fruit body form is variable with environmental conditions, and that var. phyllophyla is now treated as Marasmius bulliardii. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that Louis Krieger, writing in National Geographic in the 1920s, noted that the mushroom was used as an addition to gravies and, used to garnish venison, 'adds the appropriate touch of the wild woodlands'. This is a single-source historical remark and is not an edibility recommendation. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia says M. rotula grows in deciduous forests and fruits in groups or clusters on dead wood (especially beech), on woody debris such as twigs or sticks, and occasionally on rotting leaves. It says the fungus is widespread in North America, Europe and northern Asia, and in North America is most common in the east. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic on sticks and woody debris in hardwood forests, from spring through fall, widely distributed east of the Rocky Mountains. Mushroom World says it also occurs only occasionally on conifer wood. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, mushroomworld]*
