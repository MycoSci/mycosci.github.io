## Summary

Armillaria gallica Marxm. & Romagn. (1987) is a honey mushroom in the family Physalacriaceae, with a type locality in France and records from many countries across Europe, North America and Asia. Wikipedia describes it as a common wood-decay fungus that can live as a saprobe or as an opportunistic parasite of weakened trees. A single colony of this species in a Michigan forest, popularly called the "humongous fungus", was reported in the early 1990s as among the largest and oldest known organisms. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet gallica is botanical Latin for "French" (from Gallia, "Gaul") and refers to the type locality. It also states that the prior name bulbosa is Latin for "bulb-bearing, bulbous", and that the genus name Armillaria is derived from the Latin armilla, "bracelet". *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the taxonomy of this species was confused for a long time, in step with that of the genus Armillaria. Kari Korhonen's 1978 work separated the European Armillaria mellea complex into reproductively isolated "European Biological Species", and this species corresponds to his EBS E. In 1973 Henri Romagnesi described an Armillariella bulbosa from specimens found near Compiègne and Saint-Sauveur-le-Vicomte in France. Those specimens were later shown to be EBS E, which made the name A. bulbosa a misapplied name for it. In 1987 Romagnesi and Marxmüller renamed EBS E as Armillaria gallica. The same source says that A. lutea, described by Gillet in 1874, was rejected as a nomen ambiguum because of a lack of supporting evidence such as a specimen and a type locality. This history comes from a single encyclopedia source. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that the species is found in temperate regions of Asia, North America and Europe. It says the fungus is normally found on the ground but sometimes on stumps and logs, and that mushrooms that appear terrestrial are attached to plant roots below the surface. It describes the species as common east of the Rocky Mountains and rare in the Pacific Northwest. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) independently describes it as widely distributed east of the Rocky Mountains, growing on the ground or near the bases of hardwoods, and on the wood of hardwoods and occasionally conifers. It says the mushrooms appear alone, gregariously or in loose clusters, and that fruiting runs from late summer through fall and winter, usually late in the season. MushroomExpert.Com adds that in urban areas it can appear as a "lawn mushroom" where hardwood trees were removed several years earlier. A 2014 Mycologia abstract calls it a common opportunistic fungal pathogen of tree roots in temperate northern-hemisphere forests. These habitat statements come from separate sources, but each detail rests on one source only. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, lit_3]*
