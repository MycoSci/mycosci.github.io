## Summary

Pycnoporellus alboluteus (Ellis & Everh.) Kotl. & Pouzar, 1963, is a polypore in the family Pycnoporellaceae (order Polyporales), known in English as the orange sponge polypore. The sources describe soft, spongy, orange fruit bodies that are usually resupinate and spread across the undersides of fallen conifer logs, causing a brown cubical rot. The site catalog records it from North America and northern and eastern Europe, among other countries. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the generic name Pycnoporellus is Ancient Greek for "with countless pores", and that the epithet alboluteus combines the Latin words for "white" and "yellow". It also quotes Curtis Gates Lloyd objecting to the name because the fungus is orange, not white or yellow. This derivation comes from a single source (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both credit Ellis & Everhart, 1895, with the original description. Wikipedia says it was described as Fomes alboluteus from type specimens collected by Charles Spencer Crandall on charred trunks of Abies subalpina in the mountains of Colorado, at about 3,000 m. According to Wikipedia, it was later moved through several genera, including Polyporus, Scindalma, Aurantiporellus, Aurantiporus, Phaeolus and Hapalopilus. It received its current name in 1963, when the Czech mycologists František Kotlaba and Zdeněk Pouzar placed it in Pycnoporellus. The details of the type material and the transfer history rest on Wikipedia alone. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Both MushroomExpert.Com and Wikipedia describe a saprobic fungus on dead conifer wood, causing a brown cubical rot. It grows on the undersides of fallen logs, and Wikipedia adds stumps and logs protruding through melting snow. Hosts named by MushroomExpert.Com are spruces especially, with firs, hemlocks and pines also recorded and aspens rarely. Wikipedia names spruce (Picea) and fir (Abies) in Europe, adds poplar (Populus) in North America, and reports Picea abies and Alnus incana in Finland. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as primarily western in North America, in montane regions, and rarely encountered in the northeast; its illustrated collection was made at 11,000 feet in southwestern Colorado on Engelmann spruce. Wikipedia describes a circumpolar distribution in the boreal conifer zone, particularly the montane zone at 2,400–3,000 m. It calls the fungus abundant in the Rocky Mountains and rare in the eastern United States and Canada and in northern Europe. The two sources differ on timing. MushroomExpert.Com gives summer and fall, with the fruit bodies often surviving the winter. Wikipedia says fruit bodies begin growing under snow in spring and continue until midsummer in North America, while in Europe it is usually encountered in autumn. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
