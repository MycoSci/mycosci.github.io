## Summary

Cyathus striatus (Willd., 1787), the fluted bird's nest, is a saprobic bird's nest fungus in the Agaricaceae. Its cup-shaped fruiting body holds small spore-bearing 'eggs' (peridioles), and Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both distinguish it by a hairy or shaggy exterior and a grooved inner wall. It has been recorded across temperate regions of the world. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet derives from the Latin stria, meaning 'with fine ridges or grooves', and refers to the grooves inside the cups. Mushroom World likewise says the fluted inner surface is reflected in the Latin epithet. *[wikipedia, mushroomworld]*

## Where the common name comes from

Mushroom World says the distinctly fluted inner surface is reflected in the common name 'fluted bird's nest'. Wikipedia says the alternative common name 'splash cups' alludes to spore dispersal, in which falling water drops dislodge the peridioles from the cup. *[mushroomworld, wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that the species was first described by William Hudson in his 1778 work Flora Anglica as Peziza striata, and that Carl Ludwig Willdenow transferred it to Cyathus in 1787. No source supplied here gives further detail on the material or the reasons for the transfer. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes a saprobic fungus on decaying organic material, typically growing in clusters on small twigs or other woody debris, mostly on dead wood in open forests, and also on wood chip mulch, including in urban areas and under shrubs. It says fruiting bodies appear from summer until early winter. It adds that the fungus is more likely to be found in moist, shallow depressions than on elevated areas. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as growing scattered or gregariously on forest debris in open woods, almost never terrestrial, sometimes on woodchips, in summer and fall, and widely distributed in North America. A GBIF descriptions record lists dung, soil and decaying wood as substrates and gives the habit as coprophilous and solitary. It is based on a small set of specimen-derived entries and conflicts with the woody-debris accounts above. Wikipedia reports records from Asia, Europe, North America, Central America, South America and New Zealand. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, gbif_desc]*
