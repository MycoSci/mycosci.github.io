## Summary

Globifomes graveolens (Schwein.) Murrill, 1904, is a polypore in the family Polyporaceae and the only species in the monotypic genus Globifomes. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both describe it as a North American fungus on hardwoods, mainly oaks. The fruit body is a mass of small overlapping caps arising from a central core. Wikipedia gives the common name as sweet knot. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the generic name Globifomes combines the Latin word globus ("globe") with the name Fomes. None of the supplied sources discusses the derivation of the epithet graveolens. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

According to Wikipedia, the common name "sweet knot" refers to the odor of some specimens of freshly cut tissue, though most fruit bodies do not have this odor. MushroomExpert.Com notes that the odor is often reported by authors who did not find it themselves, and it traces this tradition possibly to Overholts (1953), who recounted his father recognizing a fresh collection as "sweet knot". Kuo's own specimens had no distinctive odor. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that the species was originally described as Boletus graveolens by Lewis David de Schweinitz in 1822, from a type specimen collected in Georgia and sent to Schweinitz for identification. William Alphonso Murrill circumscribed the genus Globifomes in 1904. Polyporus botryoides, described by Joseph-Henri Léveillé in 1846, was deemed by Murrill to be "probably not distinct" from Globifomes graveolens. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that the fungus causes a white rot in the heartwood of broadleaf trees. It is most commonly found on oak (Murrill noted a preference for water oak) and also occurs on beech, growing on both living and dead trees. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic and possibly parasitic, appearing on deadwood and from wounds of living hardwoods (primarily oaks) and causing a white rot. It gives the fruiting season as summer and fall, or year-round in warmer climates, and says the fruit bodies are annual or perennial for a few years. It reports the species as widely distributed in North America east of the Rocky Mountains, and its illustrated collection is from Ohio. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
