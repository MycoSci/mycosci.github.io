## Summary

Amanita gemmata (Fr.) Bertill. is an agaric in the family Amanitaceae, known in English as the jewelled or gemmed amanita. Wikipedia describes it as a toxic mushroom with a yellow, sticky cap bearing easily detached white warts. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that Elias Magnus Fries first described it as Agaricus gemmatus in 1838, and that Louis Bertillon transferred it to Amanita in 1866. It lists later synonyms, including Amanita muscaria var. gemmata, Amanitopsis gemmata, Amanitaria gemmata and Venenarius gemmatus. MushroomExpert.Com notes that many North American mushrooms labelled A. gemmata probably do not match the European species, and that most North American Amanita experts treat them as a group needing further study. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes it as a mycorrhizal fungus growing singly, scattered or in groups in coniferous and mixed forests, especially along paths and roads, fruiting in summer and fall. It says the species favours sandy, slightly acidic soils and is often associated with Norway spruce (Picea abies). Wikipedia cites Largent and collaborators (1980) for mycorrhizal associations with manzanita (Arctostaphylos spp.) and lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta), and a report of association with maritime pine (Pinus pinaster) in Spain. MushroomExpert.Com describes the California and Pacific Northwest form as mycorrhizal with various hardwoods and conifers, fruiting in summer, fall and winter. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

A 1994 Chilean clinical report describes four poisoning episodes in Malleco province between 1986 and 1990. Twenty-five of 36 people who ate the mushroom became ill with acute gastroenteritis. Seven went on to acute hepatitis, and one had massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Four died, three from fulminant hepatic failure and one from the bleeding. The report says A. gemmata was found in two of the episodes and an unidentified Amanita in one. It describes the clinical picture as similar to that of Amanita phalloides poisoning and notes that A. gemmata has been described as toxic in Chile since 1967.
