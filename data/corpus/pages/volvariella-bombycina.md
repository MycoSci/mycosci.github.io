## Summary

Volvariella bombycina (Schaeff.) Singer is a species in the family Pluteaceae. Schäffer first described it as Agaricus bombycinus in 1774, and Singer placed it in Volvariella in 1951. Sources describe a wood-growing mushroom with a silky white cap, free gills that are white at first and then pink, and a sac-like volva at the base of the stem. Wikipedia calls it uncommon but widespread. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the generic name Volvariella (like Volvaria and Volvariopsis, genera the species was formerly placed in) derives from the Latin volva, meaning "wrapper" or "a covering", and that the specific epithet bombycina derives from the Latin root bombyc, or "silky". This is a single-source account. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Jacob Christian Schäffer first described the species in 1774 as Agaricus bombycinus. It was later moved to Pluteus (Fries, 1836), Volvaria (Kummer, 1871) and Volvariopsis (Murrill, 1911), and Rolf Singer placed it in Volvariella in 1951. Wikipedia also says the names Amanita calyptrata (Lamarck) and Agaricus denudatus (Batsch), both published in 1783, are illegitimate because Schäffer's 1774 name has priority. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both mention a yellow variety, flaviceps, which Murrill described in 1949 from Florida. Wikipedia adds that the material grew on magnolia wood in Gainesville and that Murrill first described it as a separate species, Volvaria flaviceps. Wikipedia says Robert Shaffer later treated it as a variety of V. bombycina. MushroomExpert.Com notes that no focused, contemporary, DNA-based study of North American V. bombycina has been done, and that collections labeled with this name vary considerably in spore size, cap color and volva color. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes V. bombycina as saprobic, with fruit bodies growing singly or in small groups on trunks and decayed stumps of dead hardwoods, often in clefts and knotholes of dead or living trunks. It lists sugar maple, red maple, silver maple, magnolia, mango, beech, oak and elm as favored hosts. It also says the species has been reported fruiting in the same location for several years and, rarely, on coniferous wood. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) independently describes it as saprobic on the wood of various hardwoods, either on dead trees or from wounds of living, standing trees, in forests or urban areas. It says the mushrooms usually grow alone but sometimes gregariously, in late spring, summer and fall. Kuo calls it widely distributed in North America but much more common east of the Rocky Mountains. He says it usually appears on the wood of cultivated trees in urban settings in western areas. He also lists Central America, the Caribbean, South America, Europe and central Asia, and says it is occasionally reported from Africa and Oceania. Wikipedia lists reports from Europe, Asia (Iran, China, India, Korea and Pakistan), North and South America, Cuba and Australia, and says the species acquired protected status in Hungary in 2005. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
