## Summary

Geastrum triplex Jungh. (1840) is an earthstar in the family Geastraceae, known in English as the collared earthstar. Wikipedia describes it as the largest member of the genus, with mature fruit bodies up to 11.5 cm across when the rays are spread. Its outer layer splits into four to eight rays that lift a rounded spore sac, which often sits in a collar or saucer formed by the ray bases. MushroomExpert.Com notes that the saucer does not always form. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet triplex means "threefold" and refers to the three-layered peridium. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Franz Wilhelm Junghuhn first described the species as Geaster triplex in 1840, from a type specimen he collected on Mount Panggerangi, Java, at 910 to 1,520 m elevation. The type is now kept at the National Herbarium of the Netherlands in Leiden. Wikispecies gives the original publication as Tijdschrift voor Natuurlijke Geschiedenis en Physiologie 7: 287 (1840), under the spelling "Geaster". Wikipedia says Junghuhn distinguished it from other earthstars by the collar-like structure of the inner exoperidium layer. It also reports that Curtis Gates Lloyd wrongly suggested it was a "giant form" of G. saccatum. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both note that some mycologists treat Geastrum indicum (Klotzsch, 1832, as Cycloderma indicum) as the correct name for this species. Wikipedia adds that more recent authors argue G. indicum should be rejected as a nomen dubium and G. triplex kept. Both points are single-cluster or single-source statements. *[wikipedia, wikispecies, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia, the only source that covers this, reports that indigenous peoples of the Americas used earthstars medicinally. It says the Blackfoot called them ka-ka-toos ("fallen stars") and regarded them as an indication of supernatural events. It says the Cherokee placed fruit bodies on the navels of newborns until the umbilical cord fell off. It also reports that G. triplex is used in traditional Chinese medicine. This is a single-source account. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes G. triplex as saprobic, usually growing singly or more often in groups in hardwood forests where much humus has accumulated. It says fruit bodies are often found around well-rotted tree stumps and are at first almost buried in loose duff. In Mexico they have been collected in tropical deciduous forest. Wikipedia also says old fruit bodies persist and may survive the winter to be found the following spring or summer. It cites a Dutch study reporting a tendency to grow on calcium-rich soil from crushed shells on bicycle paths, and notes that one author states it is commonly found under beech. MushroomExpert.Com independently describes it as saprobic, growing alone or gregariously under hardwoods, in summer and fall (over winter in warmer climates), and widely distributed in North America. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
