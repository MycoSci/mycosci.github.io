## Summary

Urnula craterium (Schwein.) Fr. is a cup fungus in the family Sarcosomataceae, order Pezizales. Wikipedia describes it as having dark, goblet-shaped fruit bodies that appear in early spring. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as a regular feature of springtime woods east of the Rocky Mountains. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name means "little urn" and that the specific epithet is derived from the Latin cratera, a type of bowl used in antiquity to mix wine with water. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first described in 1822 by Lewis David de Schweinitz as Peziza craterium, from a specimen found in North Carolina. Fries described the genus Urnula in 1849 and set P. craterium as its type species. In 1896 Heinrich Rehm contentiously transferred it to Geopyxis and named Urnula terrestris as the new type species of Urnula. Elsie Kupfer's macro- and microscopic analysis showed that U. craterium was unrelated to Geopyxis, and Fries' classification was restored. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic on sticks and small logs of hardwoods, often buried so that the fruit bodies appear terrestrial. It grows alone, scattered or in dense clusters in spring and is widely distributed east of the Rocky Mountains. Wikipedia says it usually grows attached to sticks and branches, especially oak, that are partially buried in the ground, and is often found in deciduous forests. It says the species is one of the first fleshy fungi to appear, from March to May, and is sometimes encountered under melting snow. Wikipedia also states that the anamorph (Conoplea globosa) is parasitic and causes a canker of hardwoods including oaks, hickories, basswood and beech. It gives the distribution as eastern North America, Europe and Asia (Japan and China), and says the species is red-listed as critically endangered in the Czech Republic. *[mushroomexpert, wikipedia]*
