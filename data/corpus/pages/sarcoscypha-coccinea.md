## Summary

Sarcoscypha coccinea (Jacq.) Lambotte is a cup fungus in the family Sarcoscyphaceae, known as the scarlet elf cup, scarlet elf cap, scarlet cup or ruby elfcup. Wikipedia describes it as a saprobic species with a deep red, cup-shaped fruit body, widely distributed in the Northern Hemisphere. MushroomExpert.Com notes that in North America the true S. coccinea is found only in the Pacific Northwest and California, with eastern material belonging to S. dudleyi and S. austriaca. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet coccinea is derived from the Latin word meaning "deep red". *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the brilliant red interior of the cups is the source of both the common and the scientific names. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first named Helvella coccinea by Giovanni Antonio Scopoli in 1772, and Peziza coccinea by Jacquin in 1774. The genus Sarcoscypha was later conserved against Peziza, with S. coccinea as its type species. Wikipedia reports that what was long called S. coccinea in Europe and North America was shown to comprise four species (S. austriaca, S. coccinea, S. dudleyi and S. jurana), which are macroscopically indistinguishable but differ microscopically. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the Oneida people, and possibly other Iroquois tribes, used the dried, powdered fungus as a styptic, particularly on the navels of newborns. It also reports that in Scarborough, England, the fruit bodies were formerly arranged with moss and leaves and sold as table decorations. Wikipedia further states that in northern Russia the fungus is consumed in salads, fried with smetana, or used as a colored dressing, and that children in the Jura are said to eat it raw on bread and butter. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes a saprobe on decaying sticks and branches in damp spots on forest floors, often buried or partly buried, in deciduous forests. Recorded substrates include the rose family, beech, hazel, willow, elm and, in the Mediterranean, oak; a Hungarian study found it mainly on small twigs of European hornbeam. Fruiting is given as January to April, in the cooler months. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic on decaying hardwood sticks, sometimes on buried wood and appearing terrestrial, in winter and spring in California and the Pacific Northwest. Fruit bodies are described as up to 4 cm across by Kuo and 2–6 cm by Wikipedia. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
