## Summary

Clathrus archeri (Berk.) Dring, 1980 is a stinkhorn in the family Phallaceae, commonly called octopus stinkhorn or devil's fingers. It emerges from an egg-like stage and opens into several pinkish-red arms coated with a dark, foul-smelling spore slime. Its native range is disputed, and it has been recorded across Europe, Oceania, Africa and the Americas. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name Clathrus derives from the Ancient Greek κλειθρον, meaning "lattice", and that the specific epithet archeri honours the Australian architect William Archer. This derivation comes from a single source cluster. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

He calls the species the "helicopter stinkhorn" instead, borrowing the word his two-year-old son used for both an octopus and a helicopter. ## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that Miles Joseph Berkeley first described the species from a collection from Tasmania in 1859, and that it received its current binomial, Clathrus archeri, in 1980. MushroomExpert.Com cites the original authority as (Berkeley, 1860) Dring, 1980, and lists Anthurus archeri as a synonym. Wikipedia reports that New Zealand mycologist Jerry Cooper noted in 2020 that phylogenetic data do not support placing the species in Clathrus, and that he recommended the name Anthurus archeri. Wikipedia also cites a 2006 molecular analysis that found it most closely related to Aseroe rubra, Clathrus ruber, Laternea triscapa and Clathrus chrysomycelinus. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Both Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com describe it as a saprotroph (saprobe) that is common in urban areas, such as gardens and parks, near woody debris and mulch. MushroomExpert.Com adds lawns and cultivated soil, and says it grows alone or gregariously. Wikipedia states that it occurs on a variety of soil types, commonly fruits on mulch and forest litter, avoids calcareous soils, and appears to prefer acidic, humid soils. In Australia and New Zealand it has been observed fruiting on woody debris from Eucalyptus and Nothofagus trees. Wikipedia cites Sisson (2023) for a peak season of October to June in New Zealand. MushroomExpert.Com says it fruits nearly year-round depending on climate, and that its illustrated collection is from France. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
