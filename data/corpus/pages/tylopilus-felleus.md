## Summary

Tylopilus felleus (Bull.) P. Karst. (1881) is a bolete in the family Boletaceae, known in English as the bitter bolete. Wikipedia describes convex to flat brown, buff or tan caps up to 15 cm across, pores that start white and turn pinkish with age, and a coarse dark net-like pattern on the stalk. It is described as a mycorrhizal species recorded from Europe, eastern North America and east Asia. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the genus name derives from the Greek tylos "bump" and pilos "hat", and that the specific name derives from the Latin fel, "bile", referring to the bile-like bitter taste. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first described as le bolet chicotin (Boletus felleus) by the French mycologist Pierre Bulliard in 1788, and Petter Karsten transferred it in 1881 to Tylopilus, a genus diagnosed by pink spores and adnate tubes. Wikispecies also lists Boletus felleus Bull. (1788), while MushroomExpert.Com gives Bulliard's date as 1780, so the sources disagree on the year of the original description. Wikipedia says Quélet placed the taxon in Dictyopus (1886) and then Rhodoporus (1888), neither of which is recognised today. It reports that Smith and Thiers recognised var. uliginosus in 1971 on microscopic features, but Index Fungorum does not treat it as an independent taxon. Michael Kuo (MushroomExpert.Com) suspects var. uliginosus is unnecessary, because the red pigment globules in Melzer's reagent that Smith and Thiers relied on have been found to be variable. Wikipedia adds that Australian records have been classified as a separate species, T. brevisporus, because their spores are consistently smaller. MushroomExpert.Com states that the "true" T. felleus is a European species and that North American collections are probably not the same species, with the possible exception of those associated with naturalized Norway spruce. *[wikipedia, wikispecies, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes T. felleus as mycorrhizal, growing in deciduous and coniferous woodland, often under beech and oak in well-drained acid soils that can be sandy, gravelly or peaty. On calcareous soil it is said to occur in moist, waterlogged areas with ample leaf litter. Fruit bodies grow singly or in small groups, and Wikipedia says they appear from summer into autumn (June to October or even November) in many northern temperate zones. Wikipedia reports var. uliginosus from Michigan, growing among lichens and mosses under pines. MushroomExpert.Com (single source, North American collections from Pennsylvania, Michigan and Québec) and Mushroom World describe the species as mycorrhizal with conifers. MushroomExpert.Com gives the range as widely distributed east of the Rocky Mountains, growing alone, scattered or gregariously, in summer and fall. So the sources differ on host: Wikipedia lists deciduous and coniferous trees, while the two field-guide style sources list conifers. Wikipedia gives the distribution as east Asia, Europe and eastern North America, south into Mexico and Central America, and lists provinces in China and Korea. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, mushroomworld]*
