## Summary

Auriscalpium vulgare Gray (1821) is a small, stalked tooth fungus in the family Auriscalpiaceae (Russulales), known in English as the pinecone mushroom, cone tooth or ear-pick fungus. Wikipedia describes it as a saprobe fruiting on conifer cones, with a hairy brown kidney-shaped cap up to about 2 cm across, spines on the underside, and a lateral, hairy stem. It is recorded across Europe, North America and temperate Asia. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet vulgare means "common" and that the generic name Auriscalpium is Latin for "ear pick", referring to a small scoop-shaped instrument used to remove foreign matter from the ear. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Linnaeus first described the species as Hydnum auriscalpium in his 1753 Species Plantarum. In 1821 Samuel Frederick Gray judged it distinct enough from the other Hydnum species to warrant a new genus, Auriscalpium, and the name became Auriscalpium vulgare. Otto Kuntze and Howard James Banker later independently tried to restore Linnaeus' epithet, but the resulting combination (Auriscalpium auriscalpium) is a tautonym and is not validly published. Hydnum fechtneri, named by Josef Velenovský in 1922, is now considered a synonym. This history comes from a single encyclopedic source. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Saprobic on conifer cones, including cones that are partly or fully buried. Wikipedia reports that it grows solitary or clustered, typically favors Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris), has also been reported on spruce cones, and in California grows primarily on Douglas-fir cones. It describes the fungus as appearing in summer and autumn, whereas MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) gives late fall and early winter, or over winter in warmer climates, for North America, where it grows especially on pine and Douglas-fir cones alone or in groups of up to 4 or 5 per cone. A study in northeast India (Our Nature) found more basidiocarps on burned than unburned Khasi pine cones, with the number rising with cone girth on burned cones. Wikipedia also cites a study in the Laojun Mountain region of Yunnan, China, where it was among the dominant species collected from mixed forest at 2,600–3,000 m. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, lit_3]*
