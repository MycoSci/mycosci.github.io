## Summary

Omphalotus nidiformis (Berk.) O.K. Mill. 1994, the ghost fungus, is a gilled mushroom in the family Omphalotaceae, noted for its bioluminescence. Wikipedia reports it primarily from southern Australia and Tasmania, with later reports from India and Norfolk Island. Its fruit bodies are fan- or funnel-shaped, up to 30 cm across, and grow in overlapping clusters on dead or dying wood. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the epithet nidiformis derives from the Latin nidus 'nest' and forma 'shape' or 'form', hence 'nest shaped'. It also states that the synonym epithet lampas derives from the Greek lampas 'torch'. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that Miles Joseph Berkeley described it in 1844 as Agaricus nidiformis, from material collected by James Drummond in 1841 on Banksia wood along the Swan River. Berkeley also named Agaricus lampas (from material on a living Grevillea drummondii shrub) and Agaricus phosphorus (1848, from Tasmanian material collected by Ronald Campbell Gunn); these names were later synonymised with O. nidiformis. Saccardo placed all three in Pleurotus in 1887. Orson K. Miller Jr. transferred the species to Omphalotus in 1994 with other bioluminescent mushrooms. The Wikispecies record gives the basionym as Agaricus nidiformis Berk., London J. Bot. 3: 185 (1844). *[wikipedia, wikispecies]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes two disjunct ranges in southern Australia: southwest Western Australia, and the southeast of the continent from eastern South Australia through to southeast Queensland. It also occurs in Tasmania and has been recorded from Norfolk Island and, in 2012, from Kerala, India, on a coconut tree stump. It is reported from eucalypt and pine forests, arid scrubland, subalpine areas, and urban parks and gardens. It is described as a saprobe or parasite on a wide variety of hosts, including Banksia, Acacia, Eucalyptus, Nothofagus, Casuarina, and introduced Pinus and Platanus. A 2016 IMA Fungus study reports basidiomes present in winter (June-July) at its study site. *[wikipedia, lit_1]*
