## Summary

Clathrus columnatus Bosc (1811), the column stinkhorn, is a saprobic stinkhorn in the family Phallaceae. It grows from a subterranean "egg" into a fruiting body of two to five spongy, orange-to-red vertical columns joined at the apex, with a foul-smelling spore slime on the inner surfaces. It has been reported from the Americas, Africa, Australasia and China, and was first described from South Carolina. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet columnatus is Latin, meaning "supported by pillars". *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia quotes Curtis Gates Lloyd (1906) as writing that in Florida the species is known to the natives as "Dead Men's Fingers". Wikipedia adds that the name now usually refers to Xylaria polymorpha. No source narrates how the name "column stinkhorn" arose. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia says the species was first named by the French botanist Louis Augustin Guillaume Bosc in 1811. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) says it was described from South Carolina and gives an English translation of the protologue. According to Wikipedia, Nees von Esenbeck transferred it to Laternea in 1858, Cunningham to Linderia in 1932, Fischer to Colonnaria in 1933, and Cunningham to Linderiella in 1942. Wikipedia says Colonnaria, Linderia and Linderiella are now obsolete genera subsumed into Clathrus. Wikipedia also says Edward Angus Burt published a detailed account of its development in 1896. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes the species as saprobic on dead and decaying organic matter such as wood. It is often found in and around gardens and residences in mulch, wood chips or other cellulose-rich material, in sandy soil, near woody debris, and in lawns and cultivated soil. Wikipedia says fruit bodies appear singly or scattered in summer, autumn and early winter, especially after wet weather. MushroomExpert.Com (Kuo) describes it as growing alone or gregariously, often near woody debris in lawns, gardens and cultivated soil, from spring through fall, or over winter in warm climates. Kuo calls it fairly common along the Gulf Coast and says he has found it in central Illinois. Wikipedia reports it as thought to be introduced to North America because it typically appears in landscaped areas with exotic plants. A 2023 Check List paper hypothesizes that it is a recent introduction to the Galapagos Islands, where it was first confirmed. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, lit_1]*
