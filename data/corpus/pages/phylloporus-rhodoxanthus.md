## Summary

Phylloporus rhodoxanthus (Schwein.) Bres. (1900) is a species in the family Boletaceae, known in English as the gilled bolete. Unlike most boletes, it has yellow gills instead of pores. The sources place it in hardwood forests of North America, where it is mycorrhizal with living trees. *[wikipedia, inaturalist, mushroomexpert]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that the species was first described from North Carolina as Agaricus rhodoxanthus by Lewis David de Schweinitz in 1822, and that Giacomo Bresadola transferred it to Phylloporus in 1900. MushroomExpert.Com also gives North Carolina as the origin of the original description. Its author, Michael Kuo, writes that the best name for the fungus is Xerocomus rhodoxanthus but that he treats it as a Phylloporus to avoid confusion. Wikipedia adds that the name was formerly applied to Phylloporus from Asia, Australia and Europe, and that more recent research has shown those non-American records to be different species. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia states that hobbyists use the fruit bodies to make mushroom dyes in beige, greenish beige or gold, depending on the mordant used. This is a single-source statement. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes the fruit bodies as growing on the ground, singly or in small groups, in deciduous forests of oak and beech. It gives the species a wide distribution in North America, fruiting from July to October, and says it has also been reported from Belize. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as mycorrhizal with oaks and other hardwoods, growing alone or gregariously in summer, and widely distributed in North America east of the Rocky Mountains. The collections it illustrates and describes are from Illinois, Indiana and Ohio. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
