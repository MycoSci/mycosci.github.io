## Summary

Fomitopsis betulina (Bull.) B.K. Cui, M.L. Han & Y.C. Dai, 2016, is a bracket fungus in the family Fomitopsidaceae, known in English as the birch polypore, birch bracket or razor strop fungus. Sources describe it as growing on birch trees, with brackets whose caps fold over to form a smooth rim around the pore surface. It was formerly placed in Piptoporus. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet betulina refers to the genus of the host plant, Betula. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that the fungus was originally described by Jean Bulliard as Boletus betulinus, giving 1788 as the year. MushroomExpert.Com instead cites Bulliard, 1780, so the sources disagree on the year. Wikipedia says Petter Karsten transferred it to Piptoporus in 1881. It says molecular phylogenetic studies suggested the species was more closely related to Fomitopsis than to Piptoporus, and that it was reclassified to Fomitopsis in 2016. MushroomExpert.Com notes that it was originally described from France and that Piptoporus betulinus is a well-known previous name. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the velvety cut surface of the fruit body was traditionally used as a strop for finishing razor edges and as a mounting material for insect collections. It also reports use as tinder, and that the fungus was carried by Ötzi the Iceman, the 5,300-year-old mummy found in Tyrol. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes the fungus as growing almost exclusively on birch, and lists Betula pendula, B. pubescens, B. papyrifera and B. obscura as hosts. It gives the distribution as the Northern Hemisphere, including North America, Europe and Asia, and calls it a necrotrophic parasite on weakened birches. It describes brown rot and says the species is one of the most common fungi visible on dead birches. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic on the deadwood of birch, including European white birch, paper birch and yellow birch, and as sometimes reported as parasitic on living trees. It reports a brown cubical rot, growth alone or gregariously, and an annual fruiting habit. It calls the species widespread and common in Europe, western Asia and North America wherever the host trees occur, and occasionally reported from South America. Its illustrated collections are from Michigan. Both sources say the fruit bodies persist after the season they form: Wikipedia says they can last more than a year, and MushroomExpert.Com says they are often found in the next year, usually somewhat blackened. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
