## Summary

Mycena haematopus (Pers.) P. Kumm. (1871) is a species of fungus in the family Mycenaceae, order Agaricales, known in English as the bleeding fairy helmet and the burgundydrop bonnet. Wikipedia describes it as widespread and common in Europe and North America, with reddish fruit bodies that exude a dark red latex when cut or broken. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet is derived from Ancient Greek roots meaning "blood" (αἱματο-, haimato-) and "foot" (πους, pous), owing to the red latex that can easily be produced by breaking the mushroom at the base. This is a single-source statement. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that the species was first named Agaricus haematopus by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1799 and later sanctioned under that name by Elias Magnus Fries in his 1821 Systema Mycologicum. It gained its current name in 1871, when Paul Kummer raised many of Fries' Agaricus tribes, including Mycena, to the level of genus. In 1909 Franklin Sumner Earle placed it in Galactopus, a genus that is no longer considered separate from Mycena. Wikipedia also notes that Jakob Emanuel Lange described the variety marginata in 1914, and that Mitchel and Smith described the variety cuspidata in 1978 from Colorado material. MushroomExpert.Com cites the Persoon authority as 1800 rather than 1799. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes M. haematopus as saprotrophic, with fruit bodies in small groups or clusters on decaying logs, trunks and stumps of deciduous trees, particularly beech, and says it is a late colonizer that appears after white rot fungi have first decayed the wood. It reports fruit bodies year-round in mild weather, and says the species is recorded from Alaska southward in North America, is common in Europe, and has been collected in Japan and Venezuela. It also notes that in the Netherlands the species regularly fruits on ancient timber wharves. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) independently describes it as saprobic on the deadwood of hardwoods (rarely reported on conifer wood), usually on well-decayed logs without bark, in dense clusters but sometimes alone or scattered, from spring through fall (and over winter in warm climates), and widely distributed and common in North America. For the variety cuspidata, MushroomExpert.Com reports growth on the deadwood of quaking aspen in the Rocky Mountains. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
