## Summary

Leccinellum lepidum (P. Bouchet ex Essette) Bresinsky & Manfr. Binder is a bolete in the family Boletaceae, placed in the genus Leccinellum in 2003. Wikipedia describes it as a Mediterranean species that forms ectomycorrhizal associations with oaks (Quercus). The same source notes that it fruits late in the year and tolerates low temperatures. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia gives the Latin epithet lepidus as meaning "pleasant" or "charming". This is the only source that discusses the derivation. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that the species was originally described as Boletus lepidus by H. Essette in 1965. Later authors placed it in several genera. Carlo Alessio transferred it to Krombholziella in 1985, Bon treated it as a variety of Leccinum crocipodium in 1989, and Bon and Contu combined it as Leccinum lepidum in 1990. Bresinsky and Binder moved it to the newly segregated genus Leccinellum in 2003. Some authors treated it as a synonym of L. corsicum, and Wikipedia says this was later clarified. A 2019 study by Loizides and colleagues analysed collections from Corsica, Croatia, Cyprus, France and Greece. It confirmed L. lepidum, L. corsicum and L. crocipodium as distinct species within a phylogenetically validated Leccinellum. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

According to Wikipedia (the only independent source with descriptive text), the species is widespread in the Mediterranean region. It forms ectomycorrhizal associations with oaks, most commonly the evergreen "Ilex" group, particularly holm oak (Quercus ilex). Other hosts named are the golden oak (Q. alnifolia), kermes oak (Q. coccifera) and Palestine oak (Q. calliprinos). Under cork oak (Q. suber) it is described as frequently found in the western Mediterranean, and collections under Portuguese oak (Q. faginea) have also been reported. The same source says it is indifferent to substrate and occurs on both calcareous and acidic soil. It is noted for a late fruiting season and tolerance of low temperatures. In a 10-year study from Cyprus it accounted for 61% of Boletaceae collections during the winter months (December–February). *[wikipedia]*
