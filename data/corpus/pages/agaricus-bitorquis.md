## Summary

Agaricus bitorquis (Quél.) Sacc., 1887, is a species in the family Agaricaceae, known in English as the Pavement Mushroom. Sources describe a white-capped, brown-spored Agaricus whose stem carries a sheathing, often double-banded ring. It grows in hard-packed urban soil such as roadsides and paths, and is recorded pushing up through asphalt and concrete. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia gives the epithet bitorquis as Latin for "having two collars". It says the name refers to the two rings left when the veil detaches from both the top and the bottom of the stipe. This derivation comes from a single source cluster. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

iNaturalist, whose text is a declared derivative of Wikipedia, says the name "pavement mushroom" arose because the species has been recorded pushing up paving slabs. No other supplied source narrates the origin of this or any other common name. *[inaturalist]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that Lucien Quélet first defined the species in 1884 as Psalliota bitorquis, using a different genus name from the modern Agaricus. MushroomExpert.Com gives the current authority as (Quélet, 1884) Saccardo, 1887. A 1976 Journal of General Microbiology paper reports that five wild collections, originally identified as Agaricus campestris, A. edulis and A. bitorquis, proved interfertile. The authors therefore designated them all A. bitorquis (Quélet) Saccardo. Wikipedia adds that the name supersedes Agaricus rodmani. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, lit_3]*

## Cultivation

Wikipedia states that A. bitorquis was first cultivated commercially in 1968. It says cultivators have looked at the species as an alternative to the standard button mushroom, A. bisporus. The reasons it gives are greater resistance to various viral diseases, growth at higher temperatures and CO2 concentrations, and better resistance to bruising. It also says high-temperature-resistant strains have recently been developed. The 1976 Journal of General Microbiology paper reports that all its stocks could be fruited. It also raises the possibility of A. bitorquis as an alternative to A. bisporus for mass cultivation, noting an advantage in breeding. This is documented mainly by Wikipedia, a single source cluster. *[wikipedia, lit_3]*

## Habitat and ecology

MushroomExpert.Com describes the species as saprobic. It grows alone, scattered or gregariously in hard-packed soil along roadsides, near curbs, on well-worn park paths and in ditches. It is also reported, not infrequently, to arise from cracks in concrete. That source gives the fruiting season as summer and fall, or winter and spring in warm climates, and describes it as widely distributed in North America. Its illustrated collections are from Illinois, Colorado, New Mexico, Pennsylvania and Québec. It also says the species can be found throughout the northern hemisphere, usually in urban areas. Wikipedia describes it as solitary or in small groups in gardens and at roadsides, usually on the pavement, often where salt is applied against winter ice. It says the species pushes through asphalt and slabs, is subterranean and often matures underground. Wikipedia calls it occasional throughout North America, Europe, Asia and Australia. *[mushroomexpert, wikipedia]*
