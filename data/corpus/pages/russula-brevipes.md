## Summary

Russula brevipes Peck (1890) is a member of the Russulaceae, commonly called the short-stemmed russula or stubby brittlegill, with its type locality in New York. ## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the epithet brevipes derives from the Latin brevis 'short' and pes 'foot', hence 'short-footed'. MushroomExpert.Com likewise glosses brevipes as 'short-stemmed'. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that Charles Horton Peck described the species in 1890 from specimens collected in Quogue, New York. Some early 20th-century American mycologists called the North American fungus Russula delica, a name Fries had applied to a European fungus whose description did not accurately match it. Later authors (Singer, and Kühner and Romagnesi) used R. delica for other concepts. In 1964 Robert Shaffer concluded that R. brevipes was the best name for the North American collections, because it is attached to a type collection and has a reasonably explicit original description. Shaffer also defined the varieties acrior and megaspora from Californian specimens, though Index Fungorum does not treat them as independent taxa according to Wikipedia. Wikipedia adds that a 2012 publication suggested western North American R. brevipes may be a complex of at least four species. MushroomExpert.Com similarly calls R. brevipes 'as I am treating it here' essentially a combination of several or many North American species, and says DNA-based study is needed before names can be applied with confidence. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes R. brevipes as an ectomycorrhizal fungus associated with trees in the genera Abies, Picea, Pseudotsuga and Tsuga. It also reports the fungus from Pakistan's Himalayan moist temperate forests, associated with Pinus wallichiana. Wikipedia says fruit bodies grow singly or in groups, appearing July to October in eastern North America and October to January in western North America. MushroomExpert.Com, treating the name as a species group, reports it as mycorrhizal with a wide variety of trees from conifers to hardwoods, growing alone, scattered or gregariously, in summer and fall or over winter in warm climates. Its author says they find it most often in mixed woods with sandy soil in northern Michigan. A GBIF description record lists it as ectomycorrhizal, with distribution given as Vermont. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, gbif_desc]*
