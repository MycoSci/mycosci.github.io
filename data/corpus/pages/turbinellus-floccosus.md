## Summary

Turbinellus floccosus (Schwein.) Earle ex Giachini & Castellano (2011) is a cantharelloid fungus in the family Gomphaceae, known as the scaly chanterelle or scaly vase. It was called Gomphus floccosus until it was moved to Turbinellus in 2011, and it is recorded from North America and Asia. Sources conflict on whether it is safe to eat; see the disagreements section. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet is derived from the Latin floccus, meaning "tuft, or flock, of wool". *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia reports that in Nepal, in the Sherpa language, it is called diyo chyau or khumbhe chyau, from diyo ("oil lamp") and chyau ("mushroom"), because the fruit bodies are shaped like the local oil lamps. In Mexico it is called corneta or trompeta, or by the indigenous names oyamelnanácatl ("fir mushroom", from Nahuatl) and tlapitzal (from tlapitzalli, Nahuatl for "trumpet"), or tlapitzananácatl in Tlaxcala. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first described as Cantharellus floccosus by Lewis David de Schweinitz, who reported it growing in beech woods at Mount Pocono, Pennsylvania. Berkeley later named a Canadian specimen Cantharellus canadensis, and Berkeley and Curtis described a large Maine specimen as Cantharellus princeps. Franklin Sumner Earle made it the type species of Turbinellus in 1909, but that combination was not validly published. Rolf Singer transferred it to Gomphus in 1945, and E. J. H. Corner returned it to Gomphus in 1966 after Roger Heim had placed it in Nevrophyllum. In 2011 molecular analysis showed it was only distantly related to Gomphus clavatus, the type species of Gomphus, and Turbinellus was resurrected for it. MushroomExpert.Com adds that Giachini's research broadened the species concept to include Gomphus bonarii, G. canadensis and G. wilkinsae. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes it as apparently ectomycorrhizal with conifers, including Douglas-fir, fir (Abies), pine (Pinus) and western hemlock. In Mexico it is associated with Abies religiosa. It is reported from coniferous forests in North America, particularly the western states in late summer and autumn. It is most abundant in the rainy Pacific Northwest, northern California and the Sierra Nevada, and it is also recorded across Asia. MushroomExpert.Com (single-source) describes it as mycorrhizal with conifers (pines, spruces, firs, hemlocks), growing alone, scattered or gregariously, in summer and fall, or over winter along the West Coast. It was originally described from Pennsylvania and is widely distributed in northern and montane North America. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
