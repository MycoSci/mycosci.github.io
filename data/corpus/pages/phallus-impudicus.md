## Summary

Phallus impudicus L. (1753), the common stinkhorn, is a species in the family Phallaceae, order Phallales. Wikipedia describes it as recognizable by its foul odor and its phallic shape when mature, with the spore-bearing slime carried away by insects. It is recorded across much of Europe and North America, and GBIF holds occurrence records from many other countries. *[wikipedia, gbif_desc]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet impudicus is derived from the Latin for "shameless" or "immodest". MushroomExpert.Com describes Linnaeus's name as a "shameless phallus". *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that Ulisse Aldrovandi described the fungus in 1560 as "fungus priapeus", that John Gerard called it the "pricke mushroom" in 1597, and that John Parkinson called it "Hollanders workingtoole" in 1640. It says Linnaeus described it in his 1753 Species Plantarum and that it still bears its original binomial. MushroomExpert.Com and Wikispecies also give Linnaeus 1753, and Wikispecies cites Species Plantarum 2: 1179. GBIF's records list a lectotype designated by Greuter & Kuyper in 1993, which is an illustration from Micheli's Nova Plantarum Genera of 1729. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, wikispecies, gbif_desc]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia quotes Gwen Raverat's account of "stinkhorn hunting" in Victorian Cambridge, in which her aunt Etty went out with gloves and a pointed stick and burned the fungi in secret. MushroomExpert.Com retells the same story from Money. Wikipedia also says John Lightfoot wrote in 1777 that people in Thuringia called the unopened stinkhorns "ghost's or daemon's eggs", and that the species appears in Thomas Mann's novel The Magic Mountain. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Habitat and ecology

Sources differ in emphasis and give different seasons. Wikipedia says the species occurs in habitats rich in wood debris, such as forests and mulched gardens, and is associated with rotting wood. It says it is most commonly encountered in deciduous woods but may also occur in conifer woods and grassy areas such as parks and gardens, and that it may form mycorrhizal associations with certain trees. It gives the fruiting season as summer to late autumn. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic, growing alone or gregariously in gardens, flowerbeds, meadows, lawns, woodchips and cultivated areas, and also in woods in Europe. He gives the season as spring through fall, or over winter in warmer climates. Mushroom World says it grows solitary to clustered on soil, often on decaying woody debris or sawdust. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, mushroomworld]*
