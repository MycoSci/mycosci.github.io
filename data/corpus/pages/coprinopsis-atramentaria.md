## Summary

Coprinopsis atramentaria (Bull.) Redhead, Vilgalys & Moncalvo (2001) is a species in the family Psathyrellaceae, known in English as the Common Inkcap or Inky Cap. Sources describe a grey to grey-brown cap whose gills turn black and dissolve into an ink-like liquid. They also describe it as widespread across the Northern Hemisphere, growing in tufts. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet is derived from the Latin word atramentum, 'ink'. This is a single-source statement. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first described by the French naturalist Pierre Bulliard in 1786 as Agaricus atramentarius. Elias Magnus Fries placed it in the large genus Coprinus in 1838. Molecular analysis of DNA sequences later showed that most Coprinus species belonged in the family Psathyrellaceae, distinct from the type species of Coprinus, which belonged to the Agaricaceae. The species received its current binomial in 2001, when it and other species were moved to the new genus Coprinopsis. MushroomExpert.Com notes that several varieties have been described. It says var. squamosa is recognized by most authors as a separate species, Coprinopsis romagnesiana, and that Uljé (2003) treats var. acuminata as a species, Coprinus acuminatus. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia states that the black liquid the mushroom releases after being picked was once used as ink. This is a single-source statement. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes a species that grows in tufts, commonly associated with buried wood, and found in grassland, meadows, disturbed ground and open terrain. It is also common in urban areas such as vacant lots and lawns. It fruits from late spring to autumn, and tufts can be large and fruit several times a year. Fruiting bodies are reported pushing up through asphalt and tennis courts. If dug up, the mycelium can often be found originating on buried dead wood. Wikipedia places it across the Northern Hemisphere, including Eurasia and North America, with records also from South Africa and Australia. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic, growing in clusters on decaying wood (the wood may be buried, so the mushrooms appear terrestrial). It often grows from senescent roots around stumps, fruits in spring, summer and fall (sometimes winter), is frequently urban but also found in woods, and is widely distributed in North America. Mushroom World describes it as growing in tufts in fields, gardens and waste ground, near broad-leaf tree stumps or buried wood. A GBIF description record gives a Colombian collection (Tolima, 3200 m a.s.l., 23 May 2007) with the substrate listed as soil ('Suelo'). *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, mushroomworld, gbif_desc]*
