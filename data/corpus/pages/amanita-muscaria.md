## Summary

Amanita muscaria (L.) Lam., 1783, the fly agaric or fly amanita, is a species in the family Amanitaceae. It is described as a large mushroom with a bright red cap covered in white warts and white gills, native to temperate and boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere and naturalised in the Southern Hemisphere. Wikipedia notes that its genetic diversity suggests it is a species complex. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that Linnaeus described it as Agaricus muscarius in 1753, with the specific epithet deriving from Latin musca, meaning "fly". *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia reports that the common name in many European languages is thought to derive from its use as an insecticide when sprinkled in milk, a practice recorded in Germanic- and Slavic-speaking parts of Europe, the Vosges region and pockets elsewhere in France, and Romania. It also gives an alternative derivation, in which "fly" refers to the delirium caused by the fungus, based on a medieval belief that flies could enter the head and cause madness. Several regional names mean the "mad" or "fool's" version of Amanita caesarea. The derivation is presented as uncertain. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Albertus Magnus was the first to record the fly-killing use, in De vegetabilibus before 1256. Linnaeus described the species as Agaricus muscarius in Species Plantarum in 1753. Lamarck placed it in Amanita in 1783, and Fries sanctioned the name in 1821. Changes to the nomenclatural code later made Linnaeus and Lamarck the namers of Amanita muscaria (L.) Lam. Wikipedia also says Amanita muscaria is the type species of the genus. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia states that northern Eurasian shamans used A. muscaria in a non-sacred, non-obligatory, often recreational way, and that its use was culturally widespread. Marija Gimbutas reported to R. Gordon Wasson that in remote areas of Lithuania it was consumed at wedding feasts mixed with vodka. This is the only report of religious use in Eastern Europe that Wasson received. All of this comes from Wikipedia alone. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes A. muscaria as ectomycorrhizal, forming symbiotic relationships with many trees, including pine, oak, spruce, fir, birch and cedar. It is reported as native to conifer and deciduous woodlands throughout the temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere, and it has been introduced to the Southern Hemisphere with pine seedlings. Fruiting is reported in summer and autumn across most of North America, and later in autumn and early winter on the Pacific coast. iNaturalist, a declared derivative of Wikipedia, gives a similar general description. *[wikipedia, inaturalist]*
