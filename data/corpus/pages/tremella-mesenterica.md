## Summary

Tremella mesenterica is a jelly fungus in the family Tremellaceae, known in English as witches' butter and yellow brain. Its gelatinous, orange-yellow fruit body has a convoluted or lobed, brain-like form. It is recorded widely across the Northern Hemisphere and elsewhere, and it grows as a parasite on the mycelium of crust fungi in the genus Peniophora. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia gives the epithet as a Latin adjective formed from the Ancient Greek μεσεντέριον (mesentérion), 'middle intestine', from meso- ('middle, center') and énteron ('intestine'), referring to the fruit body's shape. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the name 'witches' butter' stems from Swedish and Scandinavian folklore about witchcraft. In that folklore, a bile spewed up by thieving 'carriers' or troll cats was called 'butter of the witches'. The same page notes the name is also applied to Exidia glandulosa and Fuligo septica. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was originally described in 1769 from Sweden by Anders Jahan Retzius and later sanctioned by Elias Magnus Fries in 1822. It is the type species of the genus Tremella. The form formerly recognized as Tremella lutescens is now seen as a washed-out-colour form of T. mesenterica and is treated as a synonym. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia states that in China the fungus is used by vegetarians to prepare 'an immunomodulating cooling soup with lotus seed, lily bulbs, jujube, etc.' It also says the gelatinous to rubbery consistency lends texture to soups. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia reports it most often on dead but attached and recently fallen branches, especially of angiosperms, and in bark crevices during rainy weather. It grows parasitically on the mycelium of wood-rotting corticioid fungi in the genus Peniophora. In British Columbia it is sometimes found on maple, poplar or pine but is most abundant on red alder. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it on dead hardwood sticks, often with the bark still attached, and on the decaying sticks and logs of oaks and other hardwoods. It says the fungus is usually seen in spring in temperate areas but also appears in summer, fall and winter. The illustrated collections are from Illinois and Indiana. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
