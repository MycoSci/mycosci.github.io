## Summary

Ophiocordyceps sinensis (Berk.) G.H. Sung, J.M. Sung, Hywel-Jones & Spatafora (2007) is a species in the family Ophiocordycipitaceae, known in English as caterpillar fungus and formerly placed in Cordyceps. Wikipedia describes it as an insect-pathogenic fungus that parasitizes ghost moth larvae (Hepialidae) and is primarily found in high-altitude regions of the Tibetan Plateau. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia gives the Tibetan name yartsa gunbu, literally "summer grass, winter worm", and reports that it was first recorded in the 15th century by the Tibetan doctor Zurkhar Namnyi Dorje. It says the Chinese name dōng chóng xià cǎo (冬蟲夏草) is a literal translation of the original Tibetan name. It also says the English term "vegetable caterpillar" is a misnomer because no plant is involved, and that "caterpillar fungus" is the preferred term. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Miles Berkeley first described the species in 1843 as Sphaeria sinensis. Pier Andrea Saccardo reclassified it in Cordyceps in 1878. It was known as Cordyceps sinensis until 2007, when molecular analysis led to the naming of the family Ophiocordycipitaceae and to the transfer of several Cordyceps species, including this one, to Ophiocordyceps. Wikipedia also lists Cordyceps nepalensis and C. multiaxialis as additional teleomorph synonyms, and says the presumed anamorph is Hirsutella sinensis. This history rests on a single independent source cluster. *[wikipedia, wikispecies]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that use as a folk medicine apparently originated in Tibet and Nepal. It gives the oldest known text documenting that use as one written in the late 15th century by the Tibetan doctor Zurkhar Nyamnyi Dorje (1439–1475), who describes it as an aphrodisiac. It says the first mention in traditional Chinese medicine was in Wang Ang's 1694 compendium Ben Cao Bei Yao, and that it was listed in Wu Yiluo's Ben cao cong xin in the 18th century. It also reports that the hand-collected, intact fungus-caterpillar body is valued by herbalists and that, because of its cost, its use is also a status symbol. All of this comes from one source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Cultivation

Wikipedia reports that the mycelium can be cultivated in vitro. It says industrial-scale products come from mycelia grown in liquid culture (in China) or on grains (in the West), and that no one has succeeded in rearing the fungus by infecting cultivated caterpillars for those products. It reports a fruiting body with a mature perithecium first grown in the laboratory in China in 1983, and inoculation of caterpillars leading to a mature fruiting body reported in 1991. It says that since 2016 inoculated insects can be matured in a controlled environment, and that fruiting bodies produced this way have been commercialized in China. It also says the fruiting bodies are not cultivated commercially outside China. This is a single-source account (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that the caterpillars prone to infection generally live about 15 cm underground in alpine grass and shrublands on the Tibetan Plateau and the Himalayas, at 3,000–5,000 m. It reports the fungus from the northern range of Nepal, Bhutan and the northern states of India, as well as northern Yunnan, eastern Qinghai, eastern Tibet, western Sichuan and southwestern Gansu. The host larvae belong to the family Hepialidae. The dark brown to black fruiting body grows out of the head of the dead caterpillar and emerges from the soil in alpine meadows by early spring, and spores are dispersed during late summer. Wikipedia also states that the fungus grows at comparatively low temperatures, below 21 °C. All of this is from a single source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*
