## Summary

Tolypocladium inflatum W. Gams (1971) is an ascomycete in the family Ophiocordycipitaceae (order Hypocreales). According to Wikipedia, it is most commonly encountered as a white asexual mold in soil, and it is the fungus from which the immunosuppressant drug ciclosporin was first isolated. Its sexual stage is described there as a parasite on scarab beetles. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that in 1969 a soil sample containing microfungi from Norway, collected by Hans Peter Frey, was brought to Switzerland. A fungus isolated from it was first misidentified as Trichoderma polysporum. In 1971 Walter Gams re-identified the isolate as a previously unknown microfungus and erected the genus Tolypocladium for it, naming it T. inflatum. In 1983 John Bissett re-examined the strain and found it matched Pachybasium niveum, which had been described earlier, and proposed Tolypocladium niveum. Because the fungus was economically important to the pharmaceutical industry and the name T. inflatum was already well entrenched, a proposal to conserve T. inflatum against the earlier names was made and accepted. In 1996 Kathie Hodge and colleagues determined that T. inflatum was the asexual state of what was then called Cordyceps subsessilis. That species was later moved to Elaphocordyceps and, under the 2011 'one fungus, one name' principle, was made a synonym of T. inflatum. All of this comes from a single source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that T. inflatum occurs most commonly in soil or leaf litter, particularly at high latitudes in cold soils. It also reports the species as highly tolerant of lead, and says one study found that 35% of fungal isolates recovered from lead-laden soil were T. inflatum. The sexual stage is described as forming a small ascocarp that arises from the cadaver of a host scarab beetle. Wikipedia also says this stage has been encountered as a pathogen of insects and mite pests, specifically beetle larvae and the two-spotted spider mite. A GBIF description record gives the distribution as 'Widely distributed', citing Petch 1937, Kobayasi 1982 and Sung et al. 2007, and lists NO, SE and Vermont.
