## Summary

Polyporus squamosus (Huds.) Fr., 1821, is a bracket fungus in the family Polyporaceae. Index Fungorum treats Cerioporus squamosus as the current name. The sources give the common names dryad's saddle and pheasant's back, and say it causes a white rot in the heartwood of living and dead hardwood trees. A lectin from its fruiting bodies has been purified and structurally characterised, and its biomass has been tested for Fe(III) biosorption. *[wikipedia, mushroomworld, lit_1, lit_2, lit_3]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that squamosus comes from the Latin squamosus, meaning covered with scales or scaly, and refers to the dark brown scales on the cap. This derivation comes from that one source only. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia (single source) says the name 'dryad's saddle' refers to the dryads of Greek mythology, who could conceivably sit and rest on the mushroom. It says 'pheasant's back' derives from the pattern of colours on the bracket matching that of a pheasant's back. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, William Hudson first described the species in 1778 as Boletus squamosus. Wikipedia says Lucien Quélet gave it its current name in 1886, although it is still widely known by the Elias Magnus Fries name Polyporus squamosus. Wikispecies lists Cerioporus squamosus (Huds.) Quél., 1886 among the synonyms and gives Polyporus squamosus (Huds.) Fr., 1821. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo, 2015) adds that DNA studies have shown the genus Polyporus to be incoherent and headed for major changes. Kuo says P. squamosus belongs with the P. tuberaster group. It would keep the name Polyporus if P. tuberaster is chosen as the type species, and would need a new genus name if P. brumalis is chosen. *[wikipedia, wikispecies, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia (single source) says some people value the thick, stiff paper that can be made from this and many other mushrooms of the genus Cerioporus. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic on decaying hardwood logs and stumps, and parasitic on living hardwoods, causing a white heartrot. It says the species grows alone or more often in clusters of two or three, and is annual. In the Midwest and eastern North America it grows on a wide variety of hardwoods, especially silver maple and box elder. In western North America it appears primarily on quaking aspen. It is widely distributed in North America but much more common east of the Rocky Mountains. Kuo says it is typically found in spring, but also sometimes in summer and fall, and even in winter during warm spells. Wikipedia says it is particularly common on dead elm and also found on living maple. It lists other hosts as ash, beech, horse-chestnut, Persian walnut, lime, plane, poplar, magnolia and willow. It gives fruiting seasons of July–November in much of Europe and April–October east of the Rocky Mountains in the United States. It also says the species is found in Asia and Australia. Mushroom World, an aggregator, lists Europe, North America and Asia, and names beech, elm and sycamore as favoured hosts. *[mushroomexpert, wikipedia, mushroomworld]*
