## Summary

Inonotus obliquus (Fr.) Pilát, 1942, is a fungus in the family Hymenochaetaceae, commonly called chaga. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both describe it as a parasite of birch and other trees. The conspicuous black, charcoal-like mass on the host is a sclerotium (a mass of mycelium) and not the fruiting body. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Where the common name comes from

Per Wikipedia, the name chaga comes from the Russian name of the fungus, ча́га (čága), which was in turn borrowed from the word for "mushroom" in Komi (тшак, tšak), the language of indigenous peoples of the Kama River Basin, west of the Ural Mountains. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that the Potawatomi people use the fungus, called shkitagen in their language, as a firekeeping tinder, and quotes Potawatomi biologist Robin Wall Kimmerer describing how an ember placed on it smolders slowly and holds its heat. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that it is found most commonly in the Circumboreal Region of the Northern Hemisphere, in birch forests. It grows generally on birch (Betula spp.) and has also been found on alder (Alnus spp.), beech (Fagus spp.) and poplar (Populus spp.). It causes a white heart rot, and Wikipedia says infection enters through wounds such as poorly healed branch stubs. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as parasitic on paper birch, yellow birch and other birch species, also reported on alder, and becoming saprobic after the tree dies. It says the fungus is found year-round but grows in summer and fall, and is widely distributed in northern North America across the range of its host trees. The collections illustrated and described there are from Michigan and North Carolina. Both sources say the sexual (fertile) stage appears under the bark after the tree, or part of it, has died, and is rarely seen. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
