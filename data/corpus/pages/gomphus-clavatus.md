## Summary

Gomphus clavatus (Pers.) Gray, 1821, is a member of the Gomphaceae, known in English as "pig's ears" or the violet chanterelle. Wikipedia describes a vase- or fan-shaped fruit body with an orange-brown to lilac upper surface and a wrinkled, purple spore-bearing underside. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as a mycorrhizal partner of northern and montane conifers, especially spruces and firs. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet is derived from the Latin word clava (club) and means "club-shaped", referring to the shape of young fruit bodies. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the name "pig's ears" alludes to the violet underside and yellowish cap of the fruit bodies, and notes the name is also used for Discina perlata. It also reports that in the Sherpa language of Nepal the fungus is called Eeshyamo ("mother-in-law"), because its imposing fruit body is reminiscent of a dominant mother-in-law in a Sherpa family. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, Jacob Christian Schäffer described the fungus in 1774 as Elvela (later Helvella) purpurascens. Wulfen named it Clavaria elveloides in 1781, and Persoon described it as Merulius clavatus in 1796, noting it was the species Schäffer had described. Gray transferred it to Gomphus in 1821, and because it was the first named member of the genus it became the type species. Fries later placed it in Cantharellus and then Craterellus, and other authors used Thelephora, Neurophyllum and Trombetta. Wikipedia and MushroomExpert.Com both report that, following Giachini's DNA-based work in the early 2000s, G. brevipes and G. truncatus are genetically identical to G. clavatus and may be treated as synonyms, and that G. clavatus is now considered the only Gomphus species in North America. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that Wulfen (1781) recorded that poor people ate the fungus around Klagenfurt and called it "hare's ear", and that Fries included it in his 1867 book on edible and poisonous mushrooms of Sweden. It says the species is highly regarded by the Zapotec people of Ixtlán de Juárez, Oaxaca, Mexico, and by the Sherpa people near Sagarmatha National Park, Nepal. An ethnobiological survey of Zapotec informants (95 questionnaires, 2003) found G. clavatus to be the most palatable species of those surveyed and the highest-ranked in the multifunctional food index. *[wikipedia, lit_3]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that the fungus grows on the ground, singly, in clusters or clumps, or occasionally in fairy rings. It is typically found in coniferous forests, with a preference for moist, shady areas with deep leaf litter or rotten wood debris, and is more common above about 600 m (2,000 ft) elevation. Reported mycorrhizal hosts include Abies species, Picea species, Pinus densiflora, Pseudotsuga menziesii and Tsuga heterophylla, and it is also reported with beech (Fagus sylvatica) in Europe. Wikipedia gives North American fruiting as October–December on the West Coast and July–October elsewhere. MushroomExpert.Com describes it as mycorrhizal with conifers (especially spruces and firs), growing alone, scattered or gregariously in summer and fall (or over winter on the West Coast). It was originally described from Bavaria and is widespread in Europe, and in North America it is found in northern and montane areas and the Pacific Northwest. Wikipedia also reports that it has become rare in parts of Europe, is on the national Red Lists of 17 European countries, and is regarded as extinct in the British Isles (not seen since 1927). *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
