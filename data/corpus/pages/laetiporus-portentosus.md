## Summary

Laetiporus portentosus (Berk.) Rajchenb. (1995) is a polypore fungus in the family Laetiporaceae, order Polyporales. Wikipedia reports it from South America, Australia, and the North Island and northern South Island of New Zealand. It has a documented traditional use as tinder in Australia and New Zealand. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia records that European settlers in New Zealand called the fungus "beech whiskers", "beech beard" or "morepork bread", and that it was also called "punk", a general term for any Polyporus fungus dried and used as tinder. The Māori names given are pūtawa and pangu. The source does not explain the origin of "white punk" or of the other settler names. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia states that the fungus was first described in 1844 by the English mycologist Miles Joseph Berkeley. Mario Rajchenberg transferred it to Laetiporus in 1995 on the basis of morphology. A 2008 molecular phylogeny placed it well outside the core Laetiporus clade, which the source takes to suggest that Laetiporus is polyphyletic. The source says more gene regions would need to be sequenced to resolve this. It also notes that the species has accumulated an extensive synonymy. *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

According to Wikipedia, both Australian Aboriginal peoples and New Zealand Māori traditionally used the dried fungus as tinder and for carrying fire, because it smoulders very slowly. Smouldering pieces were half-buried in the ground, carried in carved tinderboxes, or placed on a stick to light the way at night. Pākehā later used it as a fire starter and as a substitute for touchpaper, and it was sent with flint stones to New Zealand troops in both World Wars. Māori also cut it into absorbent strips and bandaged them around wounds to protect them from pressure. These uses are described in a single independent source cluster. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes the species as found in South America, Australia, and the North Island and northern South Island of New Zealand. It is usually found several metres above ground. In New Zealand it is found on southern beech (Nothofagus), and in Māori tradition it was associated with the small tree hutu (Ascarina lucida). The same source reports a wood-decay study in which the fungus decomposed the heartwood of Nothofagus pumilio strongly and the sapwood weakly. It also records a bird, the white-throated treecreeper, feeding on the fungus in heathy dry forest in Victoria, Australia. These statements come from one independent source cluster, and no season is given. *[wikipedia]*
