## Summary

Xerocomellus zelleri, known in English as Zeller's bolete, is a species in the family Boletaceae. It was first described by William Alphonso Murrill in 1912 and combined in Xerocomellus by Klofac in 2011. Wikipedia describes it as a mushroom of western North America, from British Columbia south to Mexico, with dark reddish-brown to nearly black caps, yellow pores and red-streaked yellow stems. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

According to Wikipedia, Murrill chose the specific epithet zelleri to honor Professor Sanford Myron Zeller, a mycologist at Oregon State University. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that Murrill first described the species in 1912 from specimens found on the University of Washington campus. He named it Ceriomyces zelleri, then moved it to Boletus later that year. Zeller accompanied Murrill on his Seattle expedition and found the first specimens. In 1944 Walter Henry Snell thought Xerocomus more appropriate. In 1959 Rolf Singer, Snell and Esther A. Dick transferred it to Boletellus, citing the microstructure of the trama and the faint ornamentation of the spores. In his 1976 monograph on North American boletes, Harry D. Thiers said he could not consistently find spore ornamentation and preferred to keep the species in Boletus. In 2011 it was moved to Xerocomellus. All of this comes from a single encyclopedia source (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia, the only independent source with habitat text, says the species grows solitarily or in small groups on the ground or in forest duff. It gives mature coniferous forests as the setting, often Douglas-fir forests or their margins, occasionally on grassy forest edges, and rarely on badly decayed conifer logs. It is described as ectomycorrhizal. It is said to associate with alder, poplar and other hardwoods, and to have formed ectomycorrhizae with Western Hemlock (Tsuga heterophylla) in laboratory culture. Wikipedia also notes that it has been recorded under California Redwood, sometimes in the rotted wood of old trunks, and suggests it may have saprobic tendencies. In British Columbia it is reported from summer to early winter, and infrequently in early spring. In California it is reported to fruit often after the autumn rains through March or April. Wikipedia gives the distribution as the Pacific Northwest south to California and Mexico. It says the Mexican records are from high-altitude cloud forests of Mexican Beech (Fagus mexicana), and that a report from Tibet may be a misidentification. *[wikipedia]*
