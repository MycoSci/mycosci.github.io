## Summary

Ileodictyon cibarium Tul. & C. Tul. (1844), the white basket fungus, is a member of the Phallaceae that was described from material collected at Akaroa, New Zealand. Its fruiting body develops from a white "egg" into a white, cage-like lattice, with an olive-brown spore-bearing slime on the inner surfaces of the arms. The mature lattice often detaches from its volva. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

The genus name Ileodictyon is glossed by Wikipedia as 'intestinal net', from ileo- ('intestinal') and dictyon ('net'). *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia reports an estimated 35 Māori-language names for this species, and says some of them refer to thunder (whatitiri), which may suggest the fruiting bodies appeared after storms. Examples it gives include tūtae kēhua ('ghost droppings') and tūtae whatitiri ('the faeces of Whaitiri [a mythological thunder god]'). For the English names 'basket fungus' and 'white basket fungus', iNaturalist (an aggregator declared as derived from Wikipedia) says they allude to the fruit bodies being shaped somewhat like a round or oval ball with interlaced or latticed branches. *[wikipedia, inaturalist]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was first described by Edmond Tulasne and Charles Tulasne, and the work was published by the French naval surgeon Étienne Raoul in 1844, from collections made at Akaroa, New Zealand. Wikipedia also reports that a New Zealand sand-dune Ileodictyon with a less pungent odour had been suspected to be I. gracile or an undescribed species, but DNA sequence data showed those populations also belong to I. cibarium. I. gracile was first described from Western Australia and is smaller, with thinner arms and a smooth, unwrinkled lattice (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia reports that in Māori mythology the fruiting bodies were seen as markings from atua, ancestral beings. It adds that some Māori believe the basket-like structures are the fishing nets of Taramainuku, a god associated with the Matariki star cluster. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia reports the species as naturally found in Argentina, Chile, Australia and New Zealand, and as introduced to East Africa and Southern England, likely through the transfer of soil. It says the fungus has been observed growing on soil in mulched garden beds, sand dunes, meadows and forests. MushroomExpert.Com (Michael Kuo) describes it as saprobic, growing alone or gregariously in woods or in cultivated areas and appearing year-round. It also says the species is primarily known from New Zealand and Australia, with records from Africa, Europe and South America, and that the illustrated and described collections are from New Zealand and Australia. The GBIF description record lists a distribution of Belgium, France, Great Britain and the Netherlands. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, gbif_desc]*
