## Summary

Lycoperdon echinatum Pers. (1794) is a puffball in the family Lycoperdaceae, known in English as the hedgehog puffball or spiny puffball. Wikipedia describes the fruit bodies as 2–4 cm wide and 2–3.5 cm tall, densely covered with spines up to 0.6 cm long. They are white when young and turn dark brown at maturity. The spines can fall off with age, leaving a net-like pattern of scars. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia states that the specific epithet echinatum is derived from the Greek word echinos, meaning "hedgehog" or "sea-urchin". This is a single-source statement. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikipedia, the species was later reduced to a variety of Lycoperdon gemmatum (as L. gemmatum var. echinatum) by Elias Magnus Fries. Charles Horton Peck raised it again to species level in 1879, citing the different character of its warts, its much spinier appearance, and the smoother peridium surface beneath the spines. Berkeley and Broome wrote of the fungus in 1871, but believed their specimen from Reading, Berkshire, was a new species, which they called Lycoperdon Hoylei. That name is now considered a synonym of L. echinatum, as is Utraria echinata, named by Lucien Quélet in 1873. In 1972 Vincent Demoulin described Lycoperdon americanum from a specimen found in North Carolina. Wikipedia says several authors consider it synonymous with L. echinatum, and that most authors have not followed the proposal to treat North American specimens as a separate species. All of this comes from a single source (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia (a single independent source) states that L. echinatum can be found solitary or in small groups. It says the species typically grows on the ground in deciduous forests and grassy areas, glades and pastures, on moss, humus or woody debris, with a noted preference for beech woods. Fruit bodies are said to appear from late spring to autumn. Wikipedia lists collections from eastern central Africa, China, Costa Rica, Iran, Japan and Europe, and says the species is "locally frequent" east of the Rocky Mountains in North America. For Sweden it reports that in the 1940s and 50s the species grew in beech woods with broad-leaved grasses and herbs in topsoils of pH 5.0–6.6, and that populations have since decreased owing to soil acidification. It also reports that the species is considered threatened in Åland (Finland). The GBIF record lists distribution entries for Belgium (Flemish and Walloon Regions), Denmark, Norway, Sweden and Vermont. *[wikipedia, gbif_desc]*
