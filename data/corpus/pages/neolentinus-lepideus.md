## Summary

Neolentinus lepideus (Fr.) Redhead & Ginns, 1985, is a basidiomycete in the family Gloeophyllaceae, published in Index of Fungi 5: 493 and formerly widely known as Lentinus lepideus. Sources describe it as a scaly-capped, saw-gilled, very tough-fleshed mushroom that grows on dead conifer wood, including treated lumber and railroad ties. GBIF holds occurrence records from many countries across Europe, North America and elsewhere. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, mushroomworld]*

## Where the common name comes from

Mushroom World, a single aggregator source, says the common name 'Train Wrecker' comes from the mushroom growing on old railroad ties and causing them to decompose. *[mushroomworld]*

## Discovery and naming

MushroomExpert.Com states that the species was originally described from Germany (Schaeffer 1762) and cites E. M. Fries, 1815, and S. A. Redhead & J. H. Ginns, 1985, as the authorities for the name. It notes that Lentinus lepideus is a synonym. Wikipedia likewise says the species was until recently widely known as Lentinus lepideus. *[mushroomexpert, wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia says the fruit bodies grow singly or in tufts on dead and decaying coniferous wood, favouring pines (Pinus), including old stumps, logs and timber. It adds that they may also occur in gardens, on man-made wooden structures such as old railroad ties, and in coal mines, and less frequently on non-coniferous hardwood. Wikipedia gives the fruiting season as spring to autumn, and May to November in North America (slightly shorter in the west). MushroomExpert.Com describes it as saprobic on well-decayed conifer stumps and conifer wood, including treated lumber, causing a brown rot. It says the mushroom grows alone or in small groups, in summer and fall, or year-round in warm climates. The same source calls it widely distributed in Europe and North America, also present in the Caribbean, Asia and Oceania, and reported from Africa; its illustrated collection is from Florida. Wikipedia also mentions multiple reports from the Western Cape, South Africa. MushroomExpert.Com notes that in western North America the species is more likely to appear on lumber such as fence wood and railroad tracks, while in eastern North America it appears on both natural wood and lumber. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*
