## Summary

Entoloma sinuatum (Bull.) P. Kumm. 1871 is a member of the family Entolomataceae and the type species of the genus Entoloma. Wikipedia describes it as a poisonous mushroom found across Europe and North America, and as the largest mushroom in its genus. It is recorded by Wikipedia as a cause of many mushroom poisonings in Europe. *[wikipedia]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia gives the specific epithet sinuatum as Latin for "wavy", referring to the shape of the cap. It gives the generic name as derived from the Ancient Greek entos ("inner") and loma ("fringe" or "hem"), from the inrolled margin. The older epithet lividum is given as from the Latin lividus, "lead-coloured". *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

According to Wikipedia, in the Dijon region of France the mushroom was known as le grand empoisonneur de la Côte-d'Or ("the great poisoner of Côte d'Or"). Lucien Quélet, who was himself poisoned by the fungus, called it the miller's purge, akin to the common name false miller. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia traces the name to Jean Baptiste Bulliard's Herbier de la France, where plate 382 (1788) was called Agaricus lividus. Christian Persoon described Agaricus sinuatus in 1801 from another Bulliard plate (579), and Paul Kummer transferred it to Entoloma in 1871. Quélet's 1872 "Entoloma lividus" was long used for a species whose description is generally considered different from Bulliard's. A 1950 Code change and later revisions left Persoon's name with priority. Noordeloos later found Bulliard's name illegitimate and its illustration to be a Pluteus, coined Entoloma eulividum, and then recombined it with E. sinuatum. A 1999 proposal to conserve E. lividum failed. This account rests on a single source, the Wikipedia article. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia states that fruit bodies grow solitarily or in groups and sometimes form fairy rings. They appear mainly in autumn, and also in summer in North America. In Europe the season is reported as late summer and autumn. They are found in deciduous woodland under oak, beech and, less commonly, birch, often on clay or calcareous soils. They may also occur in nearby parks, fields and grassy areas. The same source records an ectomycorrhizal relationship with willow (Salix). It gives the range as North America as far south as Arizona, throughout Europe (more common in the south and centre than the northwest), and parts of Asia including the Black Sea region, Turkey, Iran and northern Yunnan. All of this is single-source (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*
