## Summary

Phallus indusiatus Vent. (1798) is a stinkhorn in the family Phallaceae, described from Suriname. Wikipedia describes the fruit body as up to 25 cm tall, with a conical to bell-shaped cap covered in foul-smelling spore slime and a net-like skirt (indusium) hanging from beneath the cap. MushroomExpert.Com notes that many very similar Phallus species exist, and that its own page is based on photos and literature rather than the author's own study of collections. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Etymology

Wikipedia gives the specific epithet as the Latin adjective indūsǐātus, "wearing an undergarment". It gives the former generic name Dictyophora as derived from the Ancient Greek diktyon ("net") and pherō ("to bear"), hence "bearing a net". *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

According to Wikipedia, many common names are based on the species' appearance, including long net stinkhorn, crinoline stinkhorn, basket stinkhorn, bridal veil fungus and veiled lady. It says the Japanese name Kinugasatake derives from kinugasa, wide-brimmed hats with a hanging silk veil that hid and protected the wearer's face. It also says the Chinese name for "bamboo mushroom" (zhúsūn) alludes to the species' typical growth habitat. These accounts come from a single source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

Wikipedia reports that the species was described in 1798 by Étienne Pierre Ventenat and sanctioned under that name by Persoon in 1801. It quotes an anonymous 1800 publication saying the species was abundant in Dutch Guiana and had been discovered there in 1755 by the elder Vaillant. Desvaux placed it in a new genus, Dictyophora, in 1809, and it was then known for many years as Dictyophora indusiata. Nees von Esenbeck placed it in Hymenophallus in 1817. Wikipedia says both genera were eventually returned to synonymy under Phallus. MushroomExpert.Com adds that a neotype collection has been designated from Brazil (Cabral et al. 2019). *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia says a Chinese Tang dynasty pharmacopoeia ascribes traditional medicinal properties to the species, and that the Miao people of southern China continue to use it traditionally. It also says the Yoruba of Nigeria call this and other stinkhorns Akufodewa, a name it derives from Yoruba words referring to how the stench can attract hunters who mistake it for a dead animal, and that the Yoruba have reportedly used it in a charm to make hunters less visible. All of this comes from one source cluster (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Cultivation

Wikipedia reports that the species has been cultivated on a commercial scale in China since 1979. It says the substrate is agricultural waste, namely bamboo-trash sawdust covered with a thin layer of non-sterilised soil, with an optimal temperature for spawn and fruit bodies of about 24 °C and relative humidity of 90–95%. It lists other substrates that can be used: bamboo leaves and small stems, soybean pods or stems, corn stems, and willow leaves. This is a single-source account (Wikipedia). *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes the species as saprobic, growing singly or in groups in disturbed ground and among wood chips. It says the species grows in woodlands and gardens in rich soil and well-rotted woody material, and that in Asia it grows among bamboo forests and typically fruits after heavy rains. MushroomExpert.Com likewise describes it as saprobic, growing alone or gregariously in woods, especially in disturbed-ground areas such as ditches, paths and roadsides. It also calls the species common in urban settings and says it fruits year-round. The GBIF description records Brazilian specimens as solitary on soil. *[wikipedia, mushroomexpert, gbif_desc]*
