## Summary

Daldinia concentrica (Bolton) Ces. & De Not. (1863) is a fungus in the family Hypoxylaceae. Wikipedia describes it as a saprotrophic sac fungus that lives on dead and decaying wood, with a hard, shiny black, hemispherical fruiting body 2 to 7 centimeters wide, and calls it inedible. *[wikipedia]*

## Where the common name comes from

Wikipedia says the fruiting body resembles a chunk of coal, which gives it several of its common names, including coal fungus and carbon balls. It gives a legend for the name King Alfred's cake: King Alfred, hiding in a countryside homestead during war, fell asleep while minding the baking and the cakes burned, and the fungus is said to resemble a cake left to that fate. The supplied sources do not explain the name 'cramp balls'. *[wikipedia]*

## Discovery and naming

According to Wikispecies, the basionym is Sphaeria concentrica Bolton, published in 1792 (dated '1791') in the appendix to Bolton's History of Fungusses Growing about Halifax. The species was later placed in Peripherostoma (Gray, 1821), Stromatosphaeria (Greville, 1824), Hypoxylon (Greville, 1827) and Hemisphaeria (Klotzsch, 1843). The combination in Daldinia was published by Cesati & De Notaris in 1863. The supplied sources do not give the reasons for these transfers. *[wikispecies]*

## Cultural and culinary history

Wikipedia describes the fungus as a useful tinder for fire-lighting. The black variety is lighter and better than the brown, which is usually too heavy and dense. It must be completely dry, and then it takes a spark from flint and steel. It burns slowly, like a charcoal briquette, with a particularly pungent smoke. Once lit it usually needs a constant flow of oxygen to keep burning. *[wikipedia]*

## Habitat and ecology

Wikipedia describes it as a common, widespread saprotroph living on dead and decaying wood, and says it accelerates decomposition of fallen ash branches. GBIF record-level descriptions, in Spanish, give the habit as gregarious. They give the substrate as fallen logs, mainly of Quercus, as burnt trunks, and as decaying or dead wood. Distribution entries in the same GBIF data include Belgium, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Madeira Island and Vermont. *[wikipedia, gbif_desc]*
