<Aside type="danger" title="Safety Note">
  _Amanita muscaria_ is poisonous when raw and can cause severe gastrointestinal
  distress, delirium, and neurologic symptoms due to ibotenic acid and muscimol.
  Proper identification is essential—never consume red-capped mushrooms with
  white warts unless you are following rigorously researched detoxification
  protocols and local regulations allow it.
</Aside>

## 2. Discovery & Naming

- **Discoverer / Describer:** Described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as _Agaricus muscarius_.
- **Year Described:** 1753; reclassified to _Amanita_ by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck in 1783.
- **Type Locality:** Sweden; specimens cited in Linnaeus’ _Species Plantarum_.
- **Type Specimen ID:** LINN Herbarium Sheet No. 1246.7.
- **Etymology:** “Muscaria” refers to its traditional use as a fly poison (mixed with milk to stupefy houseflies).
- **Historical Notes:** Iconic in Siberian shamanism, European folklore, and global pop culture; introduced through horticulture and forestry plantings worldwide.

## 3. Morphological Characteristics

    ### Cap

    - **Shape:** Hemispherical when young, becoming convex to flat with age
    - **Size:** 8 – 20 cm diameter, occasionally larger in moist habitats
    - **Color:** Scarlet to orange-red with detachable white warts (remnants of the universal veil); cuticle often viscid in wet weather
    - **Surface:** Smooth beneath warts; margin striate when mature

    ### Gills

    - **Type:** Lamellae with plentiful short gills
    - **Attachment:** Free from the stem
    - **Color:** White to cream; may acquire pale yellowish tints with age

    ### Stem

    - **Present:** Yes, central with bulbous base
    - **Dimensions:** 8 – 20 cm × 1.5 – 3 cm, enlarging to 4 cm at bulb
    - **Characteristics:** White, adorned with a skirt-like ring; base encased in concentric rings or warted volval remnants

    ### Flesh

    - **Color & Changes:** White; bruises slowly yellowish
    - **Odor:** Mild, slightly fungal
    - **Taste:** Mild but irrelevant due to toxicity

    ### Spore Print

    - **Color:** White

## 4. Chemical Profile

- **Toxic Compounds:** Ibotenic acid (neurotoxic, causes nausea and delirium) and muscimol (potent GABA agonist producing hallucinations and sedation).
- **Medicinal Compounds:** Historically used externally for topical analgesics; contemporary research explores muscimol microdosing but remains experimental.
- **Nutritional Snapshot:** Low caloric value; proteins denatured by thorough boiling, though consumption remains controversial.
- **Secondary Metabolites:** Contains betaine, stizolobic acid, and pigment muscarufin contributing to red coloration.
- **Chemical Tests:** KOH on cap cuticle yields yellow coloration; FeSO₄ on flesh may turn olive-gray.

## 6. Ecology & Habitat

- **Habitat:** Ectomycorrhizal partner of birch, spruce, fir, pine, cedar, and occasionally oak.
- **Substrate:** Mineral soil enriched by decaying litter; often along forest edges and trails.
- **Symbiosis / Trophic Type:** Obligate biotrophic symbiont forming sheathing mycorrhizae on host tree roots.
- **Ecological Role:** Facilitates nutrient exchange between trees and soil; pioneer symbiont in disturbed forests.
- **Distribution:** Native across boreal Eurasia; widely introduced to the Southern Hemisphere and North America.
- **Altitude Range:** Sea level to subalpine zones (~2,500 m).
- **Population Status:** Abundant; occasionally invasive in Australasia and South America.

## 7. Environmental Tolerances

- **Temperature Range:** Fruiting from ~5 – 18 °C; mycelia persist under snow.
- **Moisture Needs:** Requires moist soils with consistent rainfall; fruitings follow autumn rains.
- **pH Range:** Prefers acidic to neutral soils (pH 4 – 6.5).
- **Light:** Fruits in dappled forest light; can appear in open glades near host trees.
- **USDA Zones:** Common in zones 3 – 8 where suitable host trees are present.

## 8. Cultural Notes & Detoxification Warning

<Aside type="caution" title="Traditional Use Does Not Equal Safety">
  Ethnographic reports describe traditional parboiling, drying, or fermenting to
  reduce ibotenic acid before ceremonial use. Such practices require precise
  knowledge and still carry risk. Modern toxicology advises against casual
  consumption.
</Aside>
