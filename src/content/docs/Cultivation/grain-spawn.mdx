Grain spawn is the workhorse of indoor cultivation. You hydrate and sterilize cereal grain, introduce a clean culture, and let mycelium fully colonize the kernels into a crumbly, shakeable, high-energy inoculum. That colonized grain then expands into bulk substrate or more grain. Get spawn right and the rest of the grow is mostly logistics. Get it wrong and you are just incubating contamination in a warm jar. This tek covers grain choice, the simple-up prep, hydration and the float test, jar and lid prep, sterilization, inoculation, shake-to-break, full colonization, grain-to-grain transfer, and when to spawn out.

<Badge text="Difficulty: Intermediate" /> <Badge text="Pillar: Cultivation" /> <Badge text="Prereq: Sterile Technique" />

<Aside type="caution">
Grain spawn requires true sterilization, not pasteurization, and clean transfer work. If you have not read [Sterile Technique and Working Clean](/docs/lab/sterile-technique) and [Pasteurization and Sterilization](/docs/cultivation/pasteurization-sterilization), start there. Brand-new growers should run [PF-Tek](/docs/cultivation/pf-tek) first to learn handling before committing to grain.
</Aside>

## Why grain works

Cereal grain is nutrient-dense, holds the right field-capacity moisture, and breaks apart into thousands of individual inoculation points when shaken. Each colonized kernel is a launch pad. That is why a single colonized quart can transform several pounds of bulk substrate. The spawn-to-bulk ratio is doing real work because the spawn is energetically loaded and pre-distributed.

## 1. Choosing your grain

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Rye berries">
    The default for a reason. Small kernels, large surface area, forgiving hydration window, colonizes fast and evenly. Best all-around choice.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Millet">
    Tiny kernels mean the most inoculation points per jar, which is excellent for grain-to-grain and fast, even colonization. Easy to over-hydrate, so drain hard.
  </Card>
  <Card title="WBS (wild bird seed)">
    Cheap and widely available. A mix of millet, milo, sunflower, and cracked corn. Works well, though sunflower hulls can harbor more contamination, so rinse thoroughly.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Popcorn">
    Large kernels, fewer inoculation points, slower to fully colonize but very resistant to over-hydration. Good for beginners afraid of wet grain.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

If you are unsure, use rye. Everything below assumes rye and notes where other grains differ.

## 2. The simple-up: rinse, soak, drain

The "simple-up" is the minimal-fuss prep most experienced growers settle on: a hard rinse, a soak (or a short boil), then a thorough drain to field capacity. The goal is hydrated grain that is wet inside but dry on the surface, with no standing water and no surface starch left to feed bacteria.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Soak method (overnight)">

<Steps>

1. Rinse grain in a colander under running water until the water runs clear. You are washing off dust, debris, and loose starch.

2. Transfer to a pot or bucket and cover with cool water by 2 to 3 inches. Optionally add about 1 tablespoon gypsum per pound to keep kernels from clumping.

3. Soak 12 to 24 hours at room temperature. This wakes dormant bacterial and fungal spores so sterilization can kill them.

4. Drain in the colander, then rinse again briefly. Let it drain hard for 20 to 30 minutes, shaking occasionally to shed surface water.

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Boil method (same day)">

<Steps>

1. Rinse grain until water runs clear.

2. Bring to a simmer and cook 10 to 20 minutes (rye about 15 min, popcorn about 20 min). Kernels should be hydrated but never split or butterflied. Split kernels leak starch and turn mushy.

3. Drain immediately in a colander.

4. Spread on a clean towel or tray for 15 to 30 minutes to surface-dry while still warm.

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="caution">
Burst, split, or "smiling" kernels are the number one cause of wet, contam-prone spawn. Under-cook rather than over-cook. You want kernels that are pliable and fully hydrated to the center, with intact skins.
</Aside>

### Hydration and the float test

Properly hydrated grain sits at roughly **field capacity**: saturated inside, no free water outside. Two quick checks:

- **Squeeze test:** grab a handful and squeeze. Kernels should feel firm and hydrated; at most a drop or two of water should appear. A stream means too wet.
- **Float test:** for sizing a soak, a fully hydrated kernel sinks; kernels still floating after a long soak are not yet fully hydrated. After draining, no kernel should be sitting in a puddle.

If grain came out too wet, spread it on a tray and let it surface-dry longer before jarring. Too-wet grain is the single biggest avoidable risk in this whole process, because excess free water favors heat-resistant bacteria that can survive marginal sterilization.

## 3. Jar prep, lids, and gas exchange

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Vessel">
    Wide-mouth quart or half-pint mason jars, or autoclavable polypropylene bags with a filter patch. Fill jars about two-thirds; never pack to the shoulder, because you need air space to shake.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Lids and gas exchange">
    Mycelium needs to breathe while staying contaminant-free. Drill the lid and seal with a self-healing injection port plus a micropore (medical tape) filter, or fill a larger hole with synthetic poly-fill (pillow stuffing) for filtered gas exchange.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

A common reliable lid uses one self-healing silicone injection port (for needle inoculation) and one micropore-taped hole or poly-fill hole (for gas exchange). The injection port lets you inoculate without ever opening the jar.

<Aside type="tip">
Loosen metal bands one-quarter turn before sterilizing so steam and pressure can equalize, then snug them after the cooker cools. Sealed jars can deform or shatter under pressure swings.
</Aside>

## 4. Sterilization: time and why

Grain must be **sterilized**, not pasteurized. Cereal grain carries heat-resistant bacterial endospores (notably the wet-grain bacilli that cause sour, slimy "bacterial blooms"). Pasteurization temperatures will not kill them. You need a pressure cooker or autoclave at **15 PSI (about 121 °C)**.

| Vessel | Time at 15 PSI |
| --- | --- |
| Quart jars | 90 minutes |
| Half-pint jars | 75 to 90 minutes |
| Spawn bags (full) | 2.5 to 3 hours |
| Large or deep loads | 120 minutes or more |

Time starts only after the cooker reaches full pressure. Larger, denser, or deeper grain columns need more time because heat penetrates the center slowly. The cold spot in the middle of the load is what you are actually sterilizing. When in doubt, run longer; over-sterilizing grain costs nothing but time.

<Aside type="danger">
A pressure cooker is a pressure vessel and a real burn and explosion hazard. Always use a unit with a working safety valve, never block the vent, never force the lid open while pressurized, and let it depressurize fully and cool naturally before opening. Read the manufacturer manual. Do not leave a cooker coming up to pressure unattended on a high burner.
</Aside>

Let jars cool to room temperature inside the closed cooker (often overnight). Inoculating warm jars can kill your culture, and opening a hot cooker pulls in unfiltered air as the jars cool. Both are bad.

## 5. Inoculation

Work in front of a still-air box or flow hood, with clean hands and a wiped surface. See [Sterile Technique](/docs/lab/sterile-technique) for the full protocol.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="From liquid culture / spore syringe">

<Steps>

1. Flame-sterilize the syringe needle until glowing, then let it cool a few seconds.

2. Wipe the injection port with 70% isopropyl alcohol.

3. Inject 1 to 3 mL of [liquid culture](/docs/lab/liquid-culture) per quart through the self-healing port, aiming to spread it across the grain surface.

4. Repeat across jars, re-flaming the needle between each.

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="From agar wedge">

<Steps>

1. In the still-air box, flame your scalpel and let it cool on sterile agar.

2. Open the jar only as much as needed, drop in a colonized [agar](/docs/lab/agar-work) wedge, and reseal immediately.

3. Agar transfer gives the cleanest, most vigorous genetics but requires opening the jar, so keep movements slow and inside the box.

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="From colonized grain (G2G)">
    See the grain-to-grain section below. It is the fastest method once you have a colonized jar.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="danger">
Alcohol plus open flame is a fire risk. Keep the alcohol container closed and away from the flame, never spray alcohol near an open flame, and let flamed tools cool before they touch anything flammable. Keep a clear, uncluttered workspace.
</Aside>

## 6. Incubate, shake to break, distribute

Incubate at the species-appropriate temperature. Most common cultivated species run **24 to 27 °C**. Keep jars dark and undisturbed until you see established growth.

<Steps>

1. **Wait for a foothold.** Leave jars completely still for 5 to 10 days until you see firm, rhizomorphic or healthy fluffy growth spreading from the inoculation point. Shaking too early can stall or kill a young culture.

2. **Shake to break.** Once colonization reaches roughly 20 to 40%, shake the jar vigorously to break the colonized clump apart and scatter colonized kernels evenly throughout. This multiplies inoculation points and dramatically speeds full colonization.

3. **Distribute.** Knock down clumps against your palm; aim for kernels evenly mixed, not one solid mass. If you see only one or two growth points and no spread after 10 to 14 days, suspect a failed or contaminated jar, and do not shake a suspect jar (you will aerosolize spores).

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution">
Only shake healthy jars. If you see green, black, gray, pink, or orange growth, or smell anything sour, sweet-rotten, or like wet socks, that jar is contaminated. Do not open or shake it indoors. See [Contamination Identification](/docs/lab/contamination-id).
</Aside>

## 7. Full colonization

After shaking, full colonization typically takes another 5 to 14 days depending on species, grain, and temperature. The jar is ready when **every kernel is bound in white mycelium** with no bare grain visible and no standing moisture at the bottom.

Then **consolidate**: let a fully colonized jar sit 2 to 4 extra days. The mycelium toughens, recovers its energy reserves, and the spawn performs noticeably better when spawned out. Spawning a barely-colonized jar invites a slow, weak run in bulk.

## 8. Grain-to-grain (G2G)

Grain-to-grain transfer expands one colonized jar into many fresh sterilized jars. It is the cheapest, fastest way to scale spawn.

<Steps>

1. Prepare and sterilize fresh grain jars as above, then cool fully.

2. In a still-air box or flow hood, lightly shake the colonized "master" jar to loosen kernels.

3. Pour roughly **1 part colonized grain into 5 to 10 parts fresh grain** (a colonized quart can seed several quarts). Work fast and keep jars open as briefly as possible.

4. Reseal, shake to distribute, and incubate. G2G jars often fully colonize in under a week because they start with thousands of inoculation points.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution">
Each G2G generation is a fresh chance to introduce contamination and to drift the culture. Limit open-jar G2G to a few generations off a known-clean master, and refresh your genetics from [agar or stored cultures](/docs/lab/cultures-and-storage) periodically rather than transferring grain-to-grain indefinitely.
</Aside>

## 9. Spawning windows

When do you move colonized grain to bulk or fruiting? Timing matters more than growers expect.

- **Best window:** spawn at or just after 100% colonization plus a short 2 to 4 day consolidation. The spawn is energetic, aggressive, and outruns competitors in the open bulk environment.
- **Too early:** spawning at 80 to 90% leaves bare kernels exposed to bulk-substrate contaminants before the mycelium can defend them.
- **Too late:** spawn left for weeks can dry, start primordia inside the jar, or lose vigor. Use it within a couple of weeks of full colonization, or refrigerate clean fully-colonized spawn short-term to hold it.

From here, take colonized grain to [Bulk Substrates](/docs/cultivation/bulk-substrate) and then to [Fruiting Conditions and the Monotub](/docs/cultivation/fruiting-and-monotubs).

<Aside type="danger">
Never eat anything you have not positively identified, and never consume product from a contaminated grow. Aspergillus and other molds that colonize grain produce inhalation hazards and toxins. Bag and discard contaminated jars sealed, ideally outdoors, and never open a moldy jar in your living space.
</Aside>
