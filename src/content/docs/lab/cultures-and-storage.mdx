A spore print is where a strain begins, and a well-kept culture library is how it survives. This tek covers the full backbone of preserving genetics: pulling a clean print, loading spore syringes, pouring and slanting agar, and the storage methods that keep a living culture alive for months to years. Storage is not glamorous, but it is the single highest-leverage habit in cultivation. A degraded master means every downstream grain jar, tub, and harvest inherits weakened genetics. Treat your library like the asset it is.

<Badge text="Difficulty: Intermediate" /> <Badge text="Where it fits: after agar isolation, before scaling spawn" /> <Badge text="Needs: still air box or flow hood, alcohol, foil-wrapped tools" />

<Aside type="danger">
Never taste, eat, or dose any mushroom or culture based on a print or a syringe. Spores and morphology do not confirm identity, and dangerous look-alikes exist. Misidentification kills people every year. Work in this tek is for cultivation and study only. See [Contamination Identification](/docs/lab/contamination-id) before judging any plate.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution">
Mature spores, dried mycelium, and especially contaminant molds (Aspergillus, Penicillium) release inhalable particles. Open prints, flame-sterilize, and disturb dried material near a flow hood or outdoors, never over your face. If you are immunocompromised, wear an N95 or P100 and work upwind. Aspergillus inhalation is a genuine respiratory hazard, not a hypothetical.
</Aside>

## Before you start

Everything here depends on working clean. If your sterile technique is shaky, fix that first — read [Sterile Technique & Working Clean](/docs/lab/sterile-technique) and set up a still air box (SAB) or flow hood. The methods below assume you can flame a scalpel, work in a clean airstream, and keep your hands downstream of open vessels.

<Aside type="tip">
Keep a dedicated 70% isopropyl spray bottle and a separate jar of 91%+ for flaming tools. 70% is the better surface disinfectant because the water helps it denature proteins and penetrate the cell; 91%+ evaporates fast and is what you want on a scalpel before it hits the flame.
</Aside>

## 1. Taking a clean spore print

A spore print is a deposit of spores dropped from a mature cap onto a sterile surface. Glass or foil for stock, paper only if you intend to store dry and rehydrate later.

<Steps>

1. Select a fresh, mature cap with the veil broken and gills exposed. Overripe caps drop heavily but carry more airborne contaminants; aim for a cap that has just opened.

2. Cut the cap from the stem with a flamed blade. Wipe the cap surface gently with a 70% alcohol wipe — the cap exterior, not the gills.

3. Set the cap gill-side down on flame-sterilized foil or a sterilized glass slide. A drop of sterile water on top of the cap encourages spore release.

4. Cover with a sterile cup or jar to block air currents and slow drying. Leave 2–24 hours at room temperature. A heavy print can drop in 4–6 hours; thin prints need overnight.

5. Lift the cap straight up. You should have a dense spore deposit. Fold foil prints inward without smearing, or cover a slide print with a second sterile slide.

</Steps>

A good print is dense and evenly colored. Store prints dry, folded in foil, inside a labeled zip bag with a desiccant pack. Dry spores from many species stay viable for years; some stay viable for a decade or more, though germination rates fall over time. Refrigeration extends this further. Keep prints away from humidity — moisture is what kills a stored print, by triggering partial germination and mold.

<Aside type="note">
A print is genetics in the rawest form — it is many spores, each a unique mating type. That is a feature for diversity and a problem for consistency. If you want a repeatable strain, you germinate the print, then isolate a single vigorous dikaryon on agar. See [Agar Work: Media, Plates & Isolation](/docs/lab/agar-work).
</Aside>

## 2. Making a spore syringe

A spore syringe suspends spores in sterile water for easy inoculation of grain or agar. It is the standard transport and inoculation format.

<Steps>

1. Prepare sterile water: pressure-cook distilled or RO water in a loosely capped jar at 15 psi for 15–30 minutes, or boil hard for 20 minutes and cool covered. Let it reach room temperature before use — hot water kills spores.

2. In your SAB or flow hood, scrape spores from a print into the cooled sterile water using a flame-sterilized scalpel, or stir the print directly into an open jar of sterile water.

3. Draw the suspension into a sterilized 10–20 mL syringe through a flamed 16–18 gauge needle. Cap the needle.

4. Let the loaded syringe sit 12–24 hours at room temperature so spores hydrate and disperse, then refrigerate.

</Steps>

A typical print loads several syringes. Do not over-concentrate — a faintly cloudy suspension inoculates fine and goes further. Store spore syringes in the fridge at 2–4 °C; most stay usable 6–12 months, longer if kept cold and dark. Discard any syringe that turns cloudy-yellow, smells off, or shows stringy material — that is bacterial growth.

<Aside type="danger">
Pressure cookers are scald and explosion hazards. Never open one until pressure has fully returned to zero and the lid moves freely. Keep the vent clear, never leave it unattended at pressure, and follow your unit's manual. See [Pasteurization & Sterilization](/docs/cultivation/pasteurization-sterilization) for safe operation.
</Aside>

## 3. Agar slants — the workhorse of storage

A slant is agar poured into a test tube or small jar and set at an angle to maximize surface area. Slants are the most practical long-haul storage format for the home lab: compact, sealed, and easy to revive. Inoculate from a clean isolated culture, not from spores, when you want strain consistency.

A reliable storage medium:

```
MEA storage slants (malt extract agar)
- Malt extract powder ..... 20 g
- Agar agar ............... 20 g
- Water (distilled/RO) .... 1000 mL

Mix cold, dispense ~7 mL per 15 mL tube (caps loose).
Pressure-cook at 15 psi for 30 minutes.
Cool, then set tubes at a 15 to 20 degree angle until set.
```

<Steps>

1. Sterilize tubes of media as above. Cool slanted so the agar sets at an angle with a long surface.

2. In the flow hood, transfer a small wedge of clean, actively growing mycelium from a plate onto the slant surface with a flamed scalpel. One wedge per slant.

3. Cap loosely and incubate at the species' optimum (commonly 24–27 °C for many gourmet and tropical species) until mycelium covers about half to two-thirds of the surface.

4. Tighten the cap, wipe the tube, label it, and move to cold storage before the culture fully colonizes. Storing before full colonization leaves the strain in a less stressed, more revivable state.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution">
Flaming a scalpel that still has alcohol dripping off it can ignite the alcohol jar and your work surface. Flick off excess, flame away from the open jar, and keep the alcohol capped between uses. Alcohol fires are nearly invisible in daylight. Keep the jar small, keep a lid or damp cloth within reach to smother a flame, and never reach over an open flame.
</Aside>

## 4. Storage methods and how long they last

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Fridge slants / plates">

The default. Sealed agar slants at 2–4 °C hold most species 6–18 months, many longer. Plates store too, but dry out faster and contaminate more easily — wrap plate edges in parafilm or a strip of cling film. Slants beat plates for anything past a couple of months because the deeper agar resists drying. Bring to room temperature before opening to avoid condensation on the culture.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Sterile water storage">

Excellent long-term, low-maintenance, and gentle on genetics. Drop several agar wedges of clean mycelium into a vial of sterile distilled water (no nutrients), cap, and refrigerate. With no food the culture sits near-dormant, which slows the metabolic turnover that drives degradation. Many species stay viable 2–5 years, some longer. To revive, fish out a wedge with a flamed tool and plate it. This is my preferred method for irreplaceable strains.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Grain masters">

A fully colonized grain jar or spawn bag held as a master for scaling, not long-term preservation. Refrigerated, a grain master stays usable a few weeks to a few months, but grain is nutrient-rich, so the culture keeps metabolizing and degrading. Use grain masters as working stock for [Grain Spawn](/docs/cultivation/grain-spawn), and keep the real genetic backup on slants or in water.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

<CardGrid>
<Card title="Short term (weeks to months)">
Fridge slants, plates, grain masters, spore syringes. Working stock you pull from regularly. Cheap, fast to revive, but degrades faster.
</Card>
<Card title="Long term (years)">
Sterile water storage and dry spore prints. Low metabolic activity, minimal handling. This is your insurance policy against losing a strain.
</Card>
</CardGrid>

## 5. Culture libraries and personal gene banks

A culture library is just an organized, redundant, labeled collection of your stored genetics. The discipline that separates a library from a drawer of unlabeled tubes:

- **Redundancy.** Keep at least two storage formats per strain — for example, water storage plus a backup slant. One fridge failure should never wipe a strain.
- **A master record.** A spreadsheet or notebook mapping every tube/vial/print to its strain ID, source, date, and storage method. The physical label is the index; the record is the database.
- **Periodic revival.** Even good storage is not infinite. Revive and re-store long-term cultures on a schedule — roughly every 1–2 years for slants, every 3–5 for water storage — so a strain never silently dies in the back of the fridge.
- **Provenance.** Track where a strain came from and how it was isolated. A gene bank without provenance is just clutter.

<Aside type="note">
This is the home-scale version of what culture collections do professionally. You will not have liquid-nitrogen cryostorage, and you do not need it for most cultivation work. Sterile water plus dry prints plus good records gets a hobby library to a decade-plus of reliable preservation.
</Aside>

## 6. Senescence, strain degradation, and why storage matters

Mycelium ages. Kept growing on rich media and repeatedly transferred, a strain accumulates changes — slower growth, sectoring, reduced or aborted fruiting, loss of vigor. This decline is **senescence**, and the related drift across transfers is **strain degradation**. You cannot reliably reverse it; you can only slow it and avoid accelerating it.

What accelerates degradation:

- Endless agar-to-agar transfers. Each transfer is another generation of growth and another chance to select a fast-but-weak sector.
- Storing cultures on rich, warm media where they keep metabolizing.
- Heat stress, repeated thaw/handling cycles, and contamination pressure.

What slows it:

- **Low metabolic storage.** Cold plus nutrient-poor conditions (water storage, cold slants) put the culture near-dormant. A culture that is not actively growing is barely aging.
- **Minimizing transfers.** Revive from your oldest stored backup, not from a working plate that is ten transfers deep. Keep a "generation zero" backup in deep storage and only ever expand from it.
- **Going back to spores.** When a strain finally fades, a fresh print restarts genetic diversity — though you lose the specific isolated strain and must re-isolate. This is why saving prints matters even when you have a great clone.

<Aside type="tip">
Think in generations, not calendar time. Label every culture with how many transfers it is removed from the original isolation. The number, not the date, predicts when a strain will start to fade.
</Aside>

## 7. Labeling and records

If it is not labeled, it does not exist. Every tube, vial, plate, and print gets:

- **Strain ID** — your own short code, kept consistent across the library.
- **Species** — genus and species as best identified (mark unverified ones clearly).
- **Date** stored and **generation/transfer number**.
- **Storage method** and **source** (print, clone, who/where it came from).

```
Label format (compact):
[STRAIN-ID] | species | gen N | YYYY-MM-DD | method
e.g.  OYS-A2 | P. ostreatus | gen 3 | 2026-05-31 | H2O
```

Use solvent-resistant labels or a paint pen — alcohol wipes erase ballpoint and most markers. Mirror every physical label into your master record so a smudged tube is still recoverable from the database.
