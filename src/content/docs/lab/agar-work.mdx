Agar is where serious cultivation begins. A petri dish of clear, solid media turns mycelium into something you can *see, judge, and select* — you can spot contamination before it ever reaches grain, clone the exact genetics of a fruit you liked, and breed out the weak sectors. If you only ever run spore-to-grain, you are flying blind; agar gives you eyes. This tek covers media recipes, pouring clean plates, transfers, isolation from spores and tissue, reading the leading edge, and storing your library.

Agar work assumes you already work clean. If you have not internalized still air, flame, and alcohol discipline, start there.

<LinkButton href="/docs/lab/sterile-technique" variant="secondary">Read: Sterile Technique &amp; Working Clean</LinkButton>

## Why agar at all

- **Contamination triage.** Bacteria, *Trichoderma*, and cobweb show themselves on agar days before they would on grain. You throw away a 50-cent plate instead of a jar of spawn.
- **Isolation.** A multispore syringe is a genetic shotgun. Agar lets you pick one vigorous strain and run it.
- **Cloning.** Tissue from a fruit reproduces that exact genetic, bypassing the spore lottery.
- **Stock library.** Plates and slants keep a strain alive and transferable for months.

<Aside type="note">Agar is a tool for *selection and storage*, not bulk biomass. Once you have a clean, vigorous isolate, you jump to [liquid culture](/docs/lab/liquid-culture) or [grain spawn](/docs/cultivation/grain-spawn) to scale up.</Aside>

## Media recipes

All recipes are per **1 liter** of distilled or RO water, yielding roughly 40–50 standard 100 mm plates. Agar is the gelling agent (~2%); the rest is food. Mix dry, add water, swirl, then sterilize.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="MEA (general purpose)">

Malt Extract Agar — the default workhorse. Most gourmet and many wild fungi love it.

```
20 g  light malt extract (DME or LME)
20 g  agar agar
 1 L  water
(optional) 1 g yeast / nutritional yeast for extra vigor
```

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="PDA (vigorous, fast)">

Potato Dextrose Agar — cheap, fast colonizing, slightly softer growth.

```
200 g  potato (peeled, diced, simmered 30 min; use the broth)
 20 g  dextrose (or table sugar in a pinch)
 20 g  agar agar
  1 L  total volume (top up broth to 1 L)
```

Strain the potato solids out before adding agar, or your plates will be cloudy and lumpy.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="DME + yeast (rich)">

Dried Malt Extract with yeast — a richer plate for slow or finicky strains.

```
20 g  DME (dried malt extract)
 2 g  nutritional yeast or brewer's yeast
20 g  agar agar
 1 L  water
```

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="tip">Keep agar at ~2% (20 g/L). Less and plates tear under a scalpel; more and they dry and crack. For slants in test tubes, bump to 2.5% so the slope holds.</Aside>

### Sterilizing the media

<Aside type="danger">A pressure cooker is a pressurized steam vessel. Never force the lid, never leave it unattended near dry-out, and let pressure return to zero **naturally** before opening — a sudden release can flash-boil your media and scald you. Always run with water in the base and a working pressure gauge or weight.</Aside>

<Steps>

1. Mix dry ingredients and water in a media bottle or flask. Fill no more than 2/3 — agar foams and climbs. Cap **loosely** (a quarter turn) so steam can escape.

2. Pressure cook (PC) at **15 psi for 30 minutes** for 1 L. Larger volumes need longer to reach core temperature.

3. Let pressure fall to zero on its own — typically 30–60 minutes. Do not crack the valve to rush it.

4. Move the still-molten bottle into your still air box (SAB) or flow hood and let it cool to roughly **50–55 °C** — hot enough to pour, cool enough to hold against your wrist for a couple seconds without flinching.

</Steps>

For the full sterilization rationale and times, see the dedicated tek.

## Pouring plates without condensation

Condensation is the number-one cause of ugly, contam-prone plates. The fix is **temperature control**, not luck.

<Steps>

1. Work inside a still air box or flow hood, surfaces wiped with 70% isopropyl. Flame your bottle lip if you flame-sterilize.

2. Pour at **~50 °C**, not boiling hot. Too hot and steam condenses on the lid; too cool and the agar sets while pouring.

3. Pour quickly — lift the lid only a sliver, fill each plate about **1/4 to 1/3 deep** (roughly 15–20 mL for a 100 mm plate), reseat the lid. A thin plate dries out fast; an over-full one wastes media.

4. Pop any surface bubbles with a quick pass of a flame **just above** the agar, or skip the flame if you are in still air with alcohol vapor around — see the fire warning below.

5. Leave plates to set and cool **lid-up, undisturbed** for 20–30 minutes, then **stack inverted (agar-side up)** so any condensation forms on the agar, not the lid.

</Steps>

<Aside type="danger">Isopropyl and ethanol vapor pool in a closed SAB and are **flammable**. Do not introduce an open flame into a box you just sprayed down. Either work flame-free in still air, or let alcohol fully evaporate and ventilate before lighting an alcohol lamp. People have set their gloves and their boxes on fire doing this carelessly.</Aside>

<Aside type="tip">Persistent condensation? Let set plates sit inverted at room temp for 24–48 hours before use — they self-dry. Store them in their original sleeve to slow water loss.</Aside>

## Transfers (agar to agar)

The core move: cut a colonized wedge and place it on fresh media.

<Steps>

1. Sterilize your scalpel — dip in alcohol, then flame until it glows faintly, then **let it cool 5–10 seconds** or touch it to clean agar at the plate edge. A red-hot blade cooks the tissue you are trying to move.

2. Cut a small wedge (~5 mm) from the **leading edge** of clean, actively growing mycelium — never from the old, tired center.

3. Open the fresh plate just enough, place the wedge **mycelium-side down** in the center, reseal.

4. Label the underside: strain, generation, date. Use a number system — every transfer is a new generation (G1, G2…).

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution">Each transfer adds generations and accelerates senescence (vigor loss). Keep a master plate cold and only work off subcultures. See storage below and [Spore Prints, Syringes & Culture Storage](/docs/lab/cultures-and-storage).</Aside>

## Isolating from spores

A spore swab or dilute spore solution streaked across a plate germinates into many genetically distinct colonies. Your job is to find and pick the best one.

<Steps>

1. Streak or drop a *dilute* spore suspension across the agar. Too concentrated and colonies merge into a lawn you cannot separate.

2. Incubate at the strain's preferred temp (commonly **24–27 °C** for gourmet species) until individual colonies are visible — usually 3–7 days.

3. Identify the most vigorous, rhizomorphic colony that is well-separated from its neighbors.

4. Cut its leading edge and transfer to a fresh plate. Repeat once or twice to clean it up. You now have an isolated strain.

</Steps>

## Isolating from tissue (cloning)

Cloning copies the exact genetics of a fruiting body — the same flush, same traits, no spore lottery.

<Steps>

1. Pick a **young, healthy** fruit. Wipe the outside with alcohol. The interior tissue is sterile; the surface is not.

2. Tear (do not cut through the dirty surface) the fruit open to expose clean inner flesh — the junction of cap and stem is ideal.

3. With a flamed-and-cooled scalpel, lift a tiny piece of interior tissue (~2 mm) and place it on fresh media.

4. Incubate. Within a few days mycelium radiates from the tissue. Once it is clearly clean, transfer the leading edge to a new plate.

</Steps>

<Aside type="danger">Never eat — or clone for eating — a wild fungus you have not positively identified. Cloning does not make a misidentified or contaminated specimen safe. When in doubt, throw it out.</Aside>

## Selecting and chasing the leading edge

The **leading edge** is the youngest, most active rim of growth. It is your purest, most vigorous tissue and your contamination-escape route.

- **Always transfer from the edge**, never the center. Center mycelium is older and may carry slow contaminants the edge has outrun.
- **Chase the edge** to clean a slightly contaminated plate: cut from the rim *farthest* from any contam, on the side the mycelium is racing away from it. Mycelium often outpaces bacteria; you can sometimes "rescue" a strain by repeatedly jumping ahead of the invader. If contam keeps catching up, scrap it.

<Aside type="caution">Edge-chasing is triage, not a cure. If you see fuzzy green (*Trichoderma*) or wet, shiny, foul-smelling zones (bacteria), the safe call is usually to bin the plate. Confirm what you are looking at first.</Aside>

<Aside type="danger">Do not open or sniff a contaminated or molding plate. Green and blue-green molds such as *Aspergillus* and *Penicillium* release clouds of spores when disturbed, and inhaled *Aspergillus* spores are a real respiratory hazard — especially for anyone immunocompromised, asthmatic, or pregnant. Seal a suspect plate (tape the lid shut), bag it, and dispose of it without prying it open. When you must handle visibly molding plates, do it sealed, with an N95/P2 respirator and good ventilation, and never wave the open dish toward your face to "smell-test" it.</Aside>

## Sectoring

A **sector** is a wedge of mycelium that looks different from the rest of the colony — faster, denser, more rhizomorphic, or a different texture. Sectors are natural variation, and selecting them is how cultivators breed in vigor.

- Spot a sector that grows faster or looks stronger than its neighbors.
- Cut its leading edge and isolate it on a fresh plate.
- Repeat across a few generations, always keeping the best-performing sector. This is **strain isolation** by selection.

<Aside type="note">A sudden weak, stringy, or "fluffy and going nowhere" sector can be early senescence or a degenerating strain — select *away* from it. Trust performance over appearance, but appearance is your first signal.</Aside>

### Rhizomorphic vs tomentose

Two broad growth morphologies you will learn to read:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Rhizomorphic">
    Stringy, root-like, fast, defined strands. Generally correlates with vigor and strong colonization — usually what you select *for* in gourmet/active strains.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Tomentose (fluffy)">
    Cottony, aerial, cloud-like growth. Not inherently bad — some species and many wild strains are naturally tomentose — but in species that *should* be rhizomorphic, a drift toward fluff can signal aging or off-type genetics.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="tip">Morphology is species-specific. A fluffy plate is normal for some fungi and a warning sign in others. Learn what *your* species looks like at its best, then select toward that.</Aside>

## Storing plates

A clean, sealed plate stays viable for **weeks to a few months** in the fridge. For longer storage, move to slants or a master library.

- **Short term:** seal colonized plates with a strip of micropore tape or paraffin film, store **inverted at 4 °C (fridge)**. Most mesophilic strains pause growth cold and resume when warmed. Label every plate.
- **Avoid the freezer** for plain agar plates — ice crystals rupture cells and water condenses. Long-term preservation uses slants under sterile mineral oil, or proper cryo methods, both beyond this tek.
- **Rotate your stock.** Keep one cold **master plate** untouched and only ever cut from working subcultures. Re-isolate from the master every few generations to reset vigor before senescence creeps in.

<Aside type="caution">Cold-storing a contaminated plate just slows the contam — it does not kill it. Only bank plates you are confident are clean.</Aside>
