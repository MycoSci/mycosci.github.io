Contamination is not a moral failing — it is a feedback signal. Every contam tells you something specific about your technique, your environment, or your substrate. The skill is reading that signal fast: spot it early, identify it correctly, and decide in minutes whether the dish, jar, or tub is recoverable or whether it goes in the bin before it sporulates and seeds your whole grow space. This tek is your field guide to the usual suspects — what they look like, what they smell like, and exactly what to do.

<Badge text="Difficulty: Beginner–Intermediate" /> <Badge text="Applies to: agar, grain, bulk, fruiting" />

<Aside type="danger">
The single most important rule on this page: **never eat a fruit from a contaminated grow, and never taste anything to identify it.** When in doubt, toss. No harvest is worth a hospital trip or a lungful of mold spores. Misidentification of edibility is a life-safety issue, not a yield issue.
</Aside>

## The two senses you actually use

Identification is sight plus smell. Train both.

- **Sight:** color, texture, where it starts, how fast it spreads, whether it has a defined edge or diffuse halo. Look under good raking light.
- **Smell:** healthy mycelium smells faintly mushroomy, fresh, slightly sweet — like a forest floor or fresh bread. Anything sour, rotting, fishy, like a swimming pool, like nail polish, or like a wet dog is a problem. **Do not press your nose to the substrate** and do not deep-sniff a moldy jar — waft from a distance.

<Aside type="caution">
Before you crack open a suspect container to inspect or smell it, decide where you'll open it. If you suspect black or green sporulating mold, do **not** open it indoors near your grow. Read the bag-and-bin section first.
</Aside>

## The usual suspects

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Trichoderma">

**Trichoderma (green mold)** — the most common and most aggressive killer.

- **Looks like:** starts as a bright **white**, slightly fuzzy patch that is easy to mistake for healthy mycelium. Within 24 to 72 hours it turns **forest/emerald green** from the center out as it sporulates. The white "leading edge" is the tell — healthy mycelium does not have a circular patch that greens up.
- **Smells like:** often a distinct **coconut** or musty-sweet odor.
- **Where:** grain, agar, bulk, casing — anywhere. Loves moisture and exposed nutrition.

<Badge text="Recoverable: rarely" /> Toss grain jars and tubs. On agar you can sometimes cut far ahead of it and transfer a clean wedge, but Trichoderma outruns most mycelium, so only attempt this if the genetics are irreplaceable.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Cobweb">

**Cobweb mold (Dactylium / Cladobotryum)** — fast but beatable.

- **Looks like:** wispy, **grey, diffuse, cobweb-like** growth that climbs *above* the substrate. It is fluffier and greyer than mycelium, grows much faster (visibly spreading hour to hour), and has no defined rhizomorphic strands. Advanced patches can pink up.
- **Smells like:** usually mild/musty, sometimes nothing.
- **Where:** casing layers and fruiting tubs with high humidity and stale air.

<Badge text="Recoverable: often" /> Caught early on a casing, spot-treat: mist the patch directly with **3% hydrogen peroxide**, increase fresh-air exchange, and drop surface humidity. Cobweb hates moving air and peroxide.

<Aside type="caution">
Cobweb can be mistaken for fluffy aerial mycelium or for early Trichoderma. If it is grey and spreads fast, treat as cobweb. If it greens up, it was Trichoderma and the tub is likely lost.
</Aside>

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Bacterial">

**Bacterial blotch / wet spot / sour rot** — the smell tells you first.

- **Looks like:** **wet, slimy, glistening** patches; grain that looks greasy or sweating; amber/grey goo; on caps, sunken brown blotches. Not fuzzy — bacteria are slick, not filamentous.
- **Smells like:** **sour, rotten, like burnt bacon, vomit, or old gym socks.** "Wet spot" (Bacillus) is the classic foul-smelling grain failure.
- **Where:** under-sterilized or overly wet grain, water-logged casings, condensation on caps.

<Badge text="Recoverable: no (jars), sometimes (caps)" /> Sour grain jars go straight to the bin. Surface blotch on otherwise healthy fruits is a humidity/airflow problem — fix FAE and stop misting directly on pins; the flush may still be fine, but discard slimy individual fruits.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Black / Aspergillus">

**Black mold (Aspergillus, and similar dark molds like Rhizopus)** — the dangerous one.

- **Looks like:** powdery patches that mature to **black, dark grey, brown, or yellow** depending on species. Often a pale fuzzy start with a darkening, dusty, pin-headed center. Rhizopus shows tiny black "pinheads" on tall stalks.
- **Smells like:** musty/moldy. Do not sniff to confirm.
- **Where:** anywhere, but loves warm, stagnant, nutritious conditions.

<Badge text="Recoverable: no" /> <Badge text="Health hazard" /> Toss — but **safely.** Several *Aspergillus* species are human respiratory pathogens and some produce mycotoxins. Do not open, do not disturb, do not let it sporulate in your space. See the bag-and-bin procedure below.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Penicillium">

**Penicillium (blue-green mold)** — common, less aggressive than Trichoderma.

- **Looks like:** powdery **blue-green** to grey-green, often with a clean **white border** around each colony. Frequently appears on agar and on contaminated grain as discrete fuzzy spots.
- **Smells like:** musty, sometimes faintly like blue cheese.
- **Where:** agar, grain, anything with exposed nutrition and airborne spores.

<Badge text="Recoverable: rarely" /> On agar you can occasionally rescue genetics by transferring a clean leading edge well away from any colored sporulation. Jars and bulk: toss. Treat it as a sign your spores or air are dirty.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Yeast">

**Yeast** — usually a liquid-culture and grain problem.

- **Looks like:** in liquid culture, cloudy/milky liquid instead of clear broth with discrete mycelial blobs. On grain/agar, a **wet, shiny, beige/cream, lumpy** smear that looks bacterial.
- **Smells like:** **sour, bready, beery, or like fermenting fruit.**
- **Where:** liquid culture especially, and sugar-rich substrates.

<Badge text="Recoverable: no" /> Yeast-fouled LC is done — do not inoculate from it. Bin and review your LC sterility and your inoculation technique.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Not a contaminant: overlay & stroma

Two scary-looking things that are *not* contamination — don't toss good work over them.

- **Overlay** is a dense, white, often crusty mat of mycelium that grows over the casing/substrate surface and resists pinning. It is healthy mycelium behaving badly — caused by too much humidity, too little fresh air, and high CO2. **Fix:** drop RH, ramp up FAE, and consider light scratching of the surface to trigger pinning. It is a conditions problem, not a contam.
- **Stroma** is mycelium that goes "lumpy" — a sterile, sometimes blobby/cauliflower mass that won't form normal fruits. Often genetic (an aging or unstable culture) or from excess CO2 and poor light. **Fix:** improve fruiting conditions; if it persists, the culture is degrading and you should go back to a clean stored isolate.

<Aside type="note">
Healthy aerial/tomentose mycelium (fluffy white growth reaching up) is also routinely mistaken for cobweb. The difference: aerial mycelium is white, grows from your colonizing front, and is part of the same network. Cobweb is grey and races across the surface independently. When unsure, watch it for a few hours — contams move fast.
</Aside>

## A quick decision flow

<Steps>

1. **Isolate first.** Move the suspect container away from clean cultures and your grow space *before* you open or inspect it closely. Stop airflow toward your other work.

2. **Look.** Note color, texture, defined edge versus diffuse halo, and how fast it is spreading versus the last time you checked.

3. **Waft-smell from a distance** — never deep-sniff. Sour/rotten = bacteria/yeast. Coconut = Trichoderma. Musty/dusty plus color = a sporulating mold.

4. **Classify.** Green = Trichoderma. Blue-green with white rim = Penicillium. Grey fast cobweb = Dactylium. Black/dark powder = Aspergillus/Rhizopus (hazard). Wet/slimy/sour = bacteria or yeast.

5. **Decide.** Recoverable genetics on agar means cut a clean wedge far from the contam under a still-air box or flow hood and transfer to fresh media. Everything else goes to bag-and-bin.

</Steps>

## When to bag-and-bin — and how to do it safely

Any sporulating mold (especially black/*Aspergillus*) is a spore bomb. Disturbing it indoors aerosolizes thousands of spores that will reseed your space and that you should not breathe.

<Aside type="danger">
**Aspergillus and other black/green molds can cause serious respiratory illness, including invasive infection in immunocompromised, asthmatic, or pregnant people.** Do not blow on it, do not photograph it up close indoors, do not "scoop out the bad part," and do not open it on your work surface. If you are immunocompromised, asthmatic, or pregnant, have someone else handle disposal entirely.
</Aside>

<Steps>

1. **Do not open the container.** Leave the lid (or lid-and-filter) or bag sealed. The contained mold stays contained.

2. **Mask up.** Wear at minimum an N95/FFP2 (better: a fitted P100 respirator). Add gloves.

3. **Move it outside or to a well-ventilated area** before doing anything that could release spores. Bright outdoor air with a breeze is ideal.

4. **Double-bag the entire container, sealed.** Place the whole jar/tub/dish — unopened — into a sturdy trash bag, push out excess air gently, knot it, then bag it a second time and knot again.

5. **Bin it in an outdoor or lidded trash.** Do not compost contaminated grain or substrate, and do not pour it down a drain.

6. **Decontaminate surfaces and yourself.** Wipe down the area with a disinfectant; a fresh **10% bleach** solution or 70% isopropyl works. Wash hands, and change/launder clothes if you got dusty.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution">
Never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar, or other cleaners — it releases toxic gases. Use bleach **or** isopropyl on a surface, not both at once, and ventilate while you work.
</Aside>

<Aside type="tip">
If you must open a contaminated grain bag to reuse the jar, only do it **outdoors** in a breeze, masked, after first soaking/saturating the surface to weigh down spores. Honestly, for cheap jars it is safer to just bin the whole thing.
</Aside>

## Prevention beats identification

Most contams trace back to a handful of fixable causes.

<CardGrid>
<Card title="Clean air & surfaces">
Work in front of a still-air box or flow hood, wipe everything with 70% isopropyl, and minimize traffic and dust. Most green/blue mold is airborne spores landing during inoculation. See sterile technique.
</Card>
<Card title="Full sterilization">
Bacterial wet-spot and sour grain almost always mean under-sterilized or too-wet grain. Hit a proper hold time and dial in field capacity (no free water).
</Card>
<Card title="Healthy, fast genetics">
A vigorous, fast-colonizing culture outcompetes contams. Work from clean stored isolates, not tired multi-generation transfers that drift into stroma.
</Card>
<Card title="Right conditions">
Overlay, stroma, cobweb, and surface bacteria all thrive in stagnant, over-humid air. Adequate FAE and correct RH prevent most fruiting-stage problems.
</Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="danger">
**Pressure-cooker / sterilizer safety:** never force a hot pressure cooker open. Always let it depressurize fully and the gauge return to zero before opening, keep the vent and safety valve clear, and never let a PC boil dry. A neglected sterilizer is a far bigger hazard than any mold. See the sterilization tek.
</Aside>
