Liquid culture (LC) is mycelium grown suspended in a sterile, lightly sugared water solution rather than on agar or grain. A small amount of living mycelium is added to a sterile nutrient broth, the broth is kept gently aerated or stirred, and within a week or two you have a jar of dense, fast-colonizing inoculum that can be drawn into syringes and injected into grain. Done well, LC is the single biggest speed and yield upgrade most home cultivators make after PF-Tek: one quality plate or a single syringe can be turned into liters of vigorous, genetically consistent spawn point.

<Badge text="Intermediate" /> <Badge text="Lab work" /> <Badge text="Builds on: sterile technique + agar" /> — Sits between the agar bench and grain spawn. You should already be comfortable working clean before attempting LC, because a contaminated LC jar pumps contamination straight into every grain jar you inject.

<Aside type="caution">
This tek assumes you have read [Sterile Technique &amp; Working Clean](/docs/lab/sterile-technique) and [Pasteurization &amp; Sterilization](/docs/cultivation/pasteurization-sterilization). LC is unforgiving: a broth is a perfect medium for bacteria and mold as well as for your mycelium. Most LC failures are sterile-technique failures, not recipe failures.
</Aside>

## What LC is and when to use it

LC is liquid inoculum. Its job is multiplication and delivery, not selection. Use it when you have a strain you already trust and you want a lot of it, fast.

**Use LC when:**

- You want to inoculate many grain jars from one clean source with minimal open-air exposure (a syringe through a self-healing port beats opening jars to drop in agar wedges).
- You have a verified, contaminant-free culture on agar and want to bank and bulk it.
- You are running repeat grows of the same strain and want a fast, repeatable spawn point.

**Reach for agar instead when** you need to *clean up* or *isolate* a culture — LC cannot show you what is growing in it the way a plate can. Pick your strain on agar (see [Agar Work](/docs/lab/agar-work)), then move the clean wedge into LC. Avoid using LC as your first stop straight off a wild print or a multispore swab if you can help it; you will just liquid-blend whatever contaminants came along.

<Aside type="note">
Rule of thumb: **agar selects, LC multiplies, grain bulks.** Spore/print to agar to LC to grain to bulk substrate. Each step has one job.
</Aside>

## Recipes

The target is a *light* sugar concentration — roughly **4% by mass**, i.e. about 4 g of sugar per 100 mL water, which works out to about 40 g per liter. (Note that 4 g per liter would be far too weak; you are aiming for roughly 40 g per liter.) Too rich and you encourage bacterial overgrowth and a slimy, off-smelling jar; too lean and growth is slow. Light honey, plain corn syrup (Karo), or dry malt extract (DME) all work well. Use distilled or RO water if your tap water is hard or heavily chlorinated.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Honey / Karo (simple)">

Best first recipe — cheap, forgiving, widely used.

```
1000 mL  distilled water
  40 mL  light honey OR light corn syrup (Karo)  (~4%)
```

Use *light* honey; dark honeys and raw honeys can cloud the broth and make it harder to read for contamination. Stir until fully dissolved before filling jars.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="DME (vigorous)">

Dry malt extract gives slightly faster, fluffier growth and a clearer broth.

```
1000 mL  distilled water
  40 g   dry malt extract (DME)  (~4%)
```

DME can foam during sterilization, so do not overfill jars.

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Optional additions">

Keep it simple. The only addition worth bothering with for most growers:

```
+ 1 small pinch (~0.5 g) gypsum (CaSO4) per liter
```

Gypsum buffers pH and helps mycelium clump rather than smear, which makes a cleaner draw into a syringe. Skip "secret ingredient" recipes — extra nutrients mostly feed contaminants.

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="tip">
Do not exceed about 4 to 5% sugar. Over-sugared broth is the most common reason an LC jar looks fine but throws bacterial blooms after a few days. When in doubt, go leaner.
</Aside>

## Jars, lids, ports and stir bars

A good LC jar is something you can inoculate, gas-exchange, and draw from without ever taking the lid off after sterilization.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Jar">
  Wide-mouth half-pint to pint canning jars. Half-pints make great single-use inoculum jars; pints are a good general size. Leave plenty of headspace — fill only **40–60%** so the solution can move and breathe.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Modified lid">
  Drill two holes in the metal lid: one for a **self-healing injection port** (RTV/high-temp silicone, or a purpose-made port), one for a **gas-exchange filter** (a patch of synthetic filter disc, or a tyvek/polyfill plug). The port lets you inject and withdraw with a needle; the filter lets the culture breathe without letting contaminants in.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Stir bar">
  A PTFE-coated magnetic stir bar (20–30 mm) dropped into the jar **before** sterilizing. It keeps the mycelium broken into a fine, fast-growing suspension on a magnetic stirrer.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="caution">
Silicone injection ports must be **high-temperature RTV silicone** rated to withstand pressure-cooking. Hot-glue and craft-store sealants melt or fail in the PC and will ruin a batch. Let any fresh silicone cure fully (24 hours) before sterilizing.
</Aside>

## Sterilizing the broth

LC must be **sterilized**, not merely pasteurized — a sugar broth is an ideal medium and any survivor will take over. That means a pressure cooker; boiling alone is not enough.

<Aside type="danger">
**Pressure-cooker safety.** Never leave a PC unattended at pressure. Check that the vent and safety valve are clear before every run, follow the manufacturer's fill line, and **let the cooker depressurize to zero on its own** before opening — forcing it open or cooling it too fast can cause a violent release of scalding steam. Keep face and hands clear of the vent.
</Aside>

<Steps>

1. Mix the recipe, stir until the sugar is fully dissolved, and fill jars to 40–60% with the stir bar already inside.

2. Fit the modified lids. Cover the injection port and the filter patch with a square of foil to keep condensation and stray water off them during the run.

3. Pressure-cook at **15 psi for 30 minutes** for half-pints/pints (start timing only once full pressure is reached). Larger jars need longer.

4. Kill the heat and let the cooker return to **zero pressure naturally**. Do not crack it early.

5. Move the hot jars to a still, clean, draft-free area to cool **fully to room temperature** before inoculating. Inoculating a warm jar will kill or stress the culture and pulls contaminated air in as the headspace cools.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip">
Swirl each jar gently while it cools so condensation does not pool on the filter. A soaked filter patch is an open door for contamination.
</Aside>

## Inoculating the LC

Always inoculate over a flame or in a still-air / flow hood, hands and surfaces wiped with 70% isopropyl. Flame-sterilize needles to glowing red and let them cool a few seconds before piercing the port. See [Sterile Technique](/docs/lab/sterile-technique) for the full ritual.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="From agar (best)">

The cleanest, most reliable start. A clean wedge carries a known, selected strain and almost no contamination.

<Steps>

1. Flame your scalpel, let it cool, and cut a **5–10 mm wedge** of actively growing mycelium from the leading edge of a clean plate.

2. Briefly lift the LC lid at the very edge over a flame (or work under a flow hood), drop the wedge in, and reseal immediately. Alternatively, use a flamed inoculation loop through a wide port.

3. The wedge will float and colonize outward over 5–10 days.

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="From a spore syringe">

Workable, but you are gambling on a clean print. Expect a higher contamination rate than agar, and **no strain selection** — multispore gives mixed genetics.

<Steps>

1. Shake the spore syringe well. Flame the needle, let it cool.

2. Inject **1–2 mL** through the self-healing port.

3. Germination is slower than from agar — give it 7–14 days and watch closely for any cloudiness before germination (a bad sign).

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="From existing LC">

Expanding LC to LC. Convenient, but each pass risks drift and accumulated contamination, so do not chain it endlessly.

<Steps>

1. Shake the source jar to suspend the mycelium.

2. Draw with a flamed, cooled needle and inject **1–3 mL** into the fresh jar through its port.

3. Limit serial LC-to-LC transfers; periodically return to a clean agar plate to refresh the line. See [Cultures &amp; Storage](/docs/lab/cultures-and-storage).

</Steps>

  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="danger">
You are working with **open flame and 70% alcohol** at the same time. Alcohol vapor and pooled alcohol ignite easily. Let sprayed surfaces flash off, keep the alcohol bottle capped and away from the flame, and never flame a needle directly over a wet, alcohol-soaked surface.
</Aside>

## Magnetic stirring vs shaking

The mycelium needs oxygen and needs to stay broken up. Two ways to do that:

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Magnetic stirring (preferred)">
  Set the jar on a magnetic stir plate and run the stir bar **slowly** — just enough to keep a gentle vortex, not a violent whirlpool. Periodic or low-speed stirring keeps the broth oxygenated and shears mycelium into a fine, fast suspension. Running it for a few hours a day, or continuously at low speed, both work. Result: faster, more even colonization and a cleaner draw.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Shaking (no stir plate)">
  No stirrer? Shake the jar firmly once or twice a day to break up the mycelial mass and re-suspend it. It works, just slower and less even. Avoid foaming the broth excessively.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

<Aside type="tip">
Do not over-stir. A too-fast vortex can stress or tear the mycelium and whip foam onto the filter. Slow and steady wins — a lazy swirl is plenty.
</Aside>

## Healthy vs contaminated LC

You cannot see as much in a jar as on a plate, so read it carefully and use your nose.

**Healthy LC looks like:**

- Bright white, fluffy or wispy clumps and fine cloud-like tendrils suspended through a **clear-ish** broth.
- Mycelial galaxies/snowflakes that disperse into an even cloud when shaken, then settle.
- A clean, faintly mushroomy or neutral smell.

**Contaminated LC looks like:**

- **Cloudy, turbid, milky** broth (often before any visible mycelium) — classic bacterial bloom.
- Slimy films, oily sheens, or stringy snot-like strands.
- **Off smells:** sour, rotten, cheesy, or anything sharp and unpleasant — a healthy jar never stinks.
- Colored growth: green, black, grey, pink, yellow specks — mold.
- A layer that fizzes or develops bubbles/gas.

<Aside type="danger">
**If in doubt, throw it out — sealed.** Do not open a suspect jar indoors. Mold contaminants (especially *Aspergillus*) release spores that are a genuine respiratory hazard; never sniff deeply and never sniff a clearly molded jar. Bag suspect jars sealed, and dispose of or sterilize them in the PC before opening. See [Contamination Identification](/docs/lab/contamination-id).
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution">
A turbid broth that *never* develops white mycelium is contaminated, not "just starting." When unsure, streak a drop onto an agar plate — the plate will reveal bacteria or mold within a day or two that the jar hides.
</Aside>

## Using LC to inoculate grain

This is the payoff: drawing clean inoculum into grain jars with minimal exposure. Prepare and sterilize your grain as in [Grain Spawn](/docs/cultivation/grain-spawn) first.

<Steps>

1. Confirm the LC jar is clean (white growth, clear broth, no off smell) and let it reach room temperature. Shake to suspend the mycelium evenly.

2. Working over a flame or in a flow hood, flame the syringe needle red, let it cool a few seconds, and draw **3–5 mL of LC per quart/liter of grain**.

3. Inject through the grain jar's self-healing port. Spread the dose — a little against the jar wall in two or three spots colonizes faster than one big puddle.

4. Reseal, label with strain and date, and incubate in the dark at the species' preferred temperature (commonly **24–27 °C** for many cultivated species). Keep it draft-free.

5. After a few days, shake the jar to distribute colonized grains and speed things up. Watch for any sour smell or off-color growth — pull and bag any suspect jar.

</Steps>

<Aside type="note">
Dosing is not critical, but more clean LC means faster colonization and less contamination window. With healthy LC, expect visible grain colonization within a few days and full colonization in roughly **1–2 weeks**, species-dependent. From there, move to [Bulk Substrates](/docs/cultivation/bulk-substrate) and [Fruiting Conditions](/docs/cultivation/fruiting-and-monotubs).
</Aside>

<Aside type="danger">
**Never eat anything from a misidentified or contaminated culture.** LC speed does not change the rules: positively identify your species, and if a culture or its grain ever showed signs of contamination, do not consume the fruits. When in doubt, discard.
</Aside>
