The golden chanterelle is a prized, fruity-scented edible that grows in mycorrhizal partnership with forest
trees. Its blunt, forked **false gills** — really decurrent ridges, not true gills — are the key to telling
it from its dangerous look-alikes.

<Aside type="caution" title="Safety">
  Easily confused with the toxic **jack-o'-lantern** (*Omphalotus olearius*) and the **false chanterelle**
  (*Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca*). Confirm blunt, forked, decurrent ridges (not thin true gills), growth from
  soil rather than wood, and a pale spore print before eating.
</Aside>

## Morphology

<Tabs syncKey="morphology">
  <TabItem label="Macroscopic">
    ### Cap
    - **Shape:** Convex when young, becoming funnel-shaped with a wavy margin
    - **Size:** 3–10 cm across (rarely to 15 cm)
    - **Colour:** Deep golden-yellow to egg-yolk orange; surface smooth and matte, dry even when wet

    ### Ridges (false gills) & stem
    - **Ridges:** Thick, blunt, **forked** and decurrent — running down the stem; same colour as the cap
    - **Stem:** Solid, smooth, tapering downward, 3–8 cm × 1–2 cm

    ### Flesh & spore print
    - Whitish to pale yellow flesh that does not bruise; a distinct **apricot**/fruity odour
    - Spore print white to pale cream
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Microscopic">
    - **Spores:** 7–10 × 5–6 µm, ellipsoid, smooth, thin-walled, **non-amyloid**
    - **Basidia:** 4-spored; cystidia absent
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Chemistry

- **Pigments:** Carotenoids (including canthaxanthin) give the golden colour and provide antioxidant activity.
- **Other:** Beta-glucans and sesquiterpenes; notably a good source of vitamin D2.
- **Spot tests:** No colour change with KOH or iron salts.

## Discovery & names

- Described by **Elias Fries (1821)**, who originally placed it in *Agaricus*.
- **Etymology:** *cibarius* is Latin for "of food" / "edible."
- **Names:** Golden chanterelle (EN), *girolle* (FR), *Pfifferling* (DE), *kantarell* (SE). Celebrated in European cuisine since antiquity.

## Ecology & growing conditions

- **Mode:** Ectomycorrhizal — a mutualist with spruce, pine, birch, and oak, trading soil nutrients for tree sugars. This is why it **resists cultivation**: it needs a living host and an intact forest soil community.
- **Habitat:** Moist, mossy forest floor, often along paths and clearings; sea level to ~2,000 m. Abundant where habitat is intact, but sensitive to clear-cutting.
- **Conditions:** Fruits at roughly 10–25 °C (peaking ~18 °C) on acidic soils (pH 4–6) after sustained rain.

<Steps>
1. Check the habitat — mossy conifer or mixed forest soil after rain, never on wood.
2. Examine the underside — blunt, forked, decurrent **ridges**, not thin sharp gills.
3. Take a spore print (white to cream) and rule out the wood-growing, true-gilled jack-o'-lantern.
</Steps>
